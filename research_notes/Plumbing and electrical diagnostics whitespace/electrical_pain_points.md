## 1. Most common/costly electrical symptoms and failures in commercial buildings (with numbers)

### Takeaway
Hard national data exists only for the *worst outcomes* (fires, fatalities, data-center outages), not for the everyday symptoms (nuisance trips, intermittent loads, failed contactors) where technicians lose time. Non-residential electrical-malfunction fires are about 7,400/yr and $354M/yr in the US (flat-to-slightly-declining), and data-center power failures (UPS, transfer switch, generator) are the top outage cause, but no source I found quantifies how often or how long commercial-building electrical *diagnosis* takes.

### Cited Findings
- **Fire counts and dollars.** [HARD] US Fire Administration estimates 7,400 nonresidential-building electrical-malfunction fires and $354.4M in dollar loss in 2023. The 10-year trend (2014-2023) is a 7% decrease in fires and 1% decrease in constant-dollar loss; annual fires ranged 6,800 (2020) to 8,200. USFA states deaths/injuries by individual cause are not shown because of small numbers in NFIRS. — [USFA nonresidential electrical malfunction trends 2014-2023](https://www.usfa.fema.gov/statistics/nonresidential-fires/electrical.html)
- **Older NFPA benchmark (different definition).** [HARD] NFPA (Campbell, June 2019) estimated an annual average of 16,540 non-home fires involving electrical failure or malfunction (2012-2016), with 23 civilian deaths, 200 injuries, $637M direct property damage; wiring and related equipment involved in 36%, lamps/bulbs/lighting 10%. Separately, 16,930 non-home fires involved electrical distribution and lighting equipment ($718M/yr). "Non-home" includes hotels, dormitories, care facilities; non-home residential was 18% of fires but 82% of deaths. The two NFPA counts and USFA's count use different definitions (NFPA includes structure fires broadly and electrical distribution as heat source/overload; USFA is "electrical malfunction" in nonresidential buildings) so they should not be compared directly. — [NFPA Non-Home Electrical Fires, 2019](https://content.nfpa.org/-/media/Project/Storefront/Catalog/Files/Research/NFPA-Research/Electrical/osNonHomeElectricalFires.pdf?rev=e140738968a24263af5249d2256d321d)
- **What ignited / what failed.** [HARD] In NFPA's non-home electrical distribution/lighting fires, electrical wire or cable insulation was the item first ignited in 38%; 29% first ignited part of the building; transformer or transformer fluids 3%; no single area of origin exceeded 5% (so fire data cannot localize by room). 75% involved electrical failure or malfunction as the factor, 13% equipment overloaded (Figure 3, read from chart text). — [NFPA 2019](https://content.nfpa.org/-/media/Project/Storefront/Catalog/Files/Research/NFPA-Research/Electrical/osNonHomeElectricalFires.pdf?rev=e140738968a24263af5249d2256d321d)
- **Share of big fires.** [HARD] For 2017-2019, nonresidential building fires averaged 108,500/yr, 90 deaths, 1,125 injuries, $2.8B; in 58% of nonconfined fires the fire extended beyond the room of origin, and electrical malfunction was 11% of the leading causes of those larger fires (behind "other unintentional/careless actions" 25%). Electrical is a meaningful but not dominant share of commercial fire loss. — [USFA Topical Fire Report v21i9](https://www.usfa.fema.gov/downloads/pdf/statistics/v21i9.pdf)
- **Insurer claim that "30%+ of fire losses are electrical, and electrical failure is the most likely industrial insurance claim."** [VENDOR/SNIPPET-ONLY, unsourced in the excerpt] — [Global Risk Communication thermography summary](https://riskandinsurance.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/09/GRC_A-Closer-Look-at-Infrared-Thermography.pdf). Treat as unverified; it conflicts in spirit with USFA's 11% of large nonresidential fires (different denominators).
- **Insurer view of causes.** [SECONDARY, insurer risk-engineering note, June 2022] AIG lists loose connections and overloading as typical causes of electrical overheating, and says IR surveys typically find loose connections, faulty equipment, overloaded circuits and unbalanced loads; surveys are non-contact and can be done under load. No loss statistics in the note. — [AIG Property Risk Engineering Insight: Thermography](https://www.aig.com/content/dam/aig/america-canada/us/documents/business/risk-engineering/insights/thermography-aig.pdf)
- **FM Global (all equipment breakdown, not electrical-only).** [SNIPPET-ONLY, 2018 data] Of 232 FM Global large-risk losses (>$3M) in 2018, 65 were equipment breakdown (28% of loss dollars); lack of maintenance was a factor in about two-thirds and a significant human element in nearly half. Electrical-specific split not available. — [Business Insurance summary](https://www.businessinsurance.com/Equipment-breakdown-claims-grow-in-booming-economy-FM-Global/)
- **Data-center power failures (a mission-critical commercial-electrical proxy).** [HARD, vendor-neutral industry body, survey-based] Uptime Institute's Annual Outage Analysis 2026 (May 2026): power remains the leading cause of impactful outages (45% of respondents' most recent impactful outages in 2025, down from 54% in 2024); among power-related outages (n=129, top-3 choices) the most cited causes were UPS failure, transfer switch failure and generator failure (chart percentages extracted in order: UPS 39%, utility transfer switch 34%, generator 28%, PDU 19%, A/B transfer switch 16%, single-corded IT 16%, controls 8%; label-to-number mapping inferred from text order). 57% say their last major outage cost >$100,000 and one in five >$1M. Uptime says failures are "less often the result of a single point of failure" and more tied to complex interactions, making root-cause analysis harder. — [Uptime Annual Outage Analysis 2026 (PDF)](https://datacenter.uptimeinstitute.com/rs/711-RIA-145/images/2026.AnnualOutageAnalysis.pdf); press release: [Uptime 2025 report](https://uptimeinstitute.com/about-ui/press-releases/uptime-announces-annual-outage-analysis-report-2025)
- **Human/procedural factor in those outages.** [HARD for the 2026 text; note inconsistency] Uptime 2026: "human error" is a contributing factor in nine of 10 outages. The 2025 release says failure to follow procedures rose 10 points and 85% of human-error incidents stem from not following procedures or flawed procedures; a 2025 trade-blog write-up of the same Uptime work reports 58% instead of 85%, so the figure is inconsistent between secondary sources. — [Uptime 2025 press](https://uptimeinstitute.com/about-ui/press-releases/uptime-announces-annual-outage-analysis-report-2025); [DPS Telecom summary](https://www.dpstele.com/blog/2025-outage-report-changing-risks.php)
- **Grid-outage cost context (out of scope, grid-side).** [SNIPPET-ONLY] LBNL estimates sustained power interruptions cost US customers about $44B/yr (2015$), ~70% borne by commercial customers; includes huge uncertainty. This is utility-side outage cost, not in-building diagnosis, so it overstates the addressable pain. — [LBNL](https://emp.lbl.gov/news/berkeley-lab-estimates-sustained)
- **Emergency generator/ATS reliability claims.** [VENDOR, REJECTED] A CMMS vendor guide claims "ATS failure rates increase 3x within 5 years," "37% of hospital generator failures are fuel degradation," and a 99.87% generator start reliability attributed to NREL. I fetched the page: it names no sources or links for any of its statistics. Do not use. — [OxMaint hospital generator guide](https://oxmaint.com/industries/healthcare/hospital-emergency-generator-maintenance-nfpa-110-guide). Uptime (above) is the only credible data I found for ATS/generator failure share.
- **Joint Commission (hospital) documentation/maintenance findings.** [SNIPPET-ONLY, low confidence] Search-result summaries (from compliance-vendor pages) state EC.02.05.01 (utility systems) and EC.02.06.01 were each cited in more than 60% of hospital surveys in the FY2025 report, that EC.02.05.01's utility-labeling element (including "electrical breaker schedule accuracy") was scored at ~61%, and EC.02.05.07 (emergency power testing documentation) ~40%. I could not open a primary Joint Commission table. — [OxMaint Joint Commission guide](https://oxmaint.com/industries/healthcare/joint-commission-maintenance-compliance-guide); [Mills/Joint Commission 2024 slide deck (unreadable PDF)](https://cdn.ymaws.com/nehes.org/resource/resmgr/conference/2024/doc_mills_george100324.pdf)
- **Lighting controls.** [SNIPPET-ONLY] A 2007 Heschong Mahone field study reportedly found only 25% of sidelighting photocontrols working properly and 52% not operating at all (disabled after complaints, never commissioned, or incompatible with the BMS); a 2009 LBNL commissioning meta-study reportedly found >35% of existing buildings had lighting deficiencies. Dated (2007/2009) and from an unopened page. — [Electrical Contractor magazine](https://www.ecmag.com/magazine/articles/article-detail/lighting-commissioning-and-retrocommissioning-next-step-lighting-controls). A 2018 engineer's list of why lighting-control commissioning fails (unclear sequences, multi-vendor integration with BAS, compressed schedules, no post-occupancy follow-up) contains no statistics. [ANECDOTE/expert opinion] — [Consulting-Specifying Engineer, Nov 2018](https://www.csemag.com/10-reasons-lighting-controls-commissioning-goes-bad/)
- **EV chargers (mostly public/network chargers, not building distribution).** [HARD, peer-reviewed preprint] UC Berkeley (Rempel et al., 2022): of 657 DC fast-charger connectors at 181 Bay Area stations, 72.5% functional; non-functioning causes (22.7%) were unresponsive/unavailable screens, payment failures, charge-initiation failures, network failures, broken connectors; 4.9% had cables too short; operators claimed 95-98% uptime. — [arXiv 2203.16372](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.16372). [SECONDARY] J.D. Power 2025 EVX study (7,428 owners, Jan-Jun 2025): 14% of charging visits failed (19% in 2024), with a broken/out-of-service charger the cause in 60% of failures. — [EVChargingStations summary](https://evchargingstations.com/chargingnews/j-d-power-ev-chargers-becoming-more-reliable/). Failures are dominated by software/payment/network/connector faults, which are OEM-network diagnostics rather than building electrical diagnostics.
- **Power quality (harmonics, neutral, nuisance trips).** [SNIPPET-ONLY/VENDOR] Search summaries describe third-harmonic neutral overload, nuisance tripping from distorted waveforms, and an IEEE paper on RCD nuisance tripping from high neutral-to-ground voltage in commercial buildings, but I found no prevalence study. — [IEEE Xplore, RCD nuisance tripping at commercial buildings](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/6738980/); [ECS International LED harmonics guide (vendor)](https://www.ecsintl.com/the-impact-of-harmonics-on-led-lighting-in-commercial-buildings-a-2026-power-quality-guide/)
- **Loose connections/thermal hot spots.** [VENDOR/SNIPPET-ONLY] Claims that "76% of electrical anomalies found are loose or deteriorated connections" and that surveys average $14,000 savings for eight findings come from thermography-vendor pages without clear primary sources. — [Oxmaint thermography blog](https://oxmaint.com/blog/post/blog-post-infrared-thermography-electrical-maintenance) (listed in search results; not opened). Plausible qualitatively (AIG corroborates loose connections as a typical cause) but not quantifiable.
- **Preventive maintenance savings.** [SECONDARY] A 2026 trade article cites US DOE as saying preventive programs can cut maintenance costs 12-18% vs reactive. — [Buildings magazine, Apr 2026](https://www.buildings.com/building-systems-om/maintenance/article/55372402/the-power-of-preventive-maintenance-meeting-new-nfpa-70b-requirements-in-2026). The same page's electrical fire/fatality numbers match USFA/ESFI.
- **An often-quoted "77% of distribution failures are preventable" figure** appears in a search summary attributed loosely to USFA; I could not trace a primary source. [UNVERIFIED] — [EC&M NFPA 70B article (opened; contains no external stats)](https://www.ecmweb.com/test-measurement/article/55252392/nfpa-70b-and-the-resurgence-of-the-electrical-maintenance-program)

### Inferences
- The best-quantified electrical pain is *consequence* (fires, injuries, data-center outages), not *time-to-diagnose*; any pitch built on "electrical diagnosis wastes X hours" lacks hard backing in public sources.
- Failure types that are naturally "symptom -> cause" problems (nuisance trips/ground faults, intermittent loads, neutral/harmonics, lighting-control misbehavior) are the ones with no national datasets, which is itself a whitespace signal and a validation risk.
- Data-center evidence (UPS/transfer switch/generator, procedure failure) suggests the highest-dollar, best-documented diagnostic domain is mission-critical power (also hospitals), where there are monitored points and strict change control, which is a different customer from "commercial building technician."

### Gaps
- No primary dataset on frequency of nuisance trips, ground faults, intermittent faults in commercial buildings.
- No credible generator/ATS/emergency-lighting failure-rate study found (only an unsourced vendor guide and Uptime data-center data). NFPA 110/NFPA 99 test-failure data not located.
- No VFD, contactor, or transformer failure-cause data located for commercial buildings.
- FM Global's electrical-specific loss statistics and EPRI/IEEE Gold Book (IEEE 493) failure statistics not retrieved.

## 2. Trial-and-error, circuit tracing, repeat visits; first-time-fix data for electrical

### Takeaway
There is no electrical-specific first-time-fix dataset in public sources; the only numbers are generic field-service benchmarks (about 75-77% FTFR across trades, from vendor/analyst citations). Practitioner testimony strongly says finding the fault is the time sink and the fix is quick, and that intermittent faults and circuit identification are the hardest cases.

### Cited Findings
- **Generic FTFR benchmarks.** [VENDOR/SNIPPET-ONLY] Cited figures: The Service Council ~77% for field service, Aberdeen Group ~75% (one in four calls needs a repeat visit); top-20% performers 88%+; for electrical contractors a vendor blog says "aim for 70-85%," which is a target not a measurement. Aberdeen reportedly attributes most failed first fixes to missing/incorrect parts, not diagnosis, so FTFR does not isolate diagnostic failure. — [FieldProxy electrical KPIs](https://www.fieldproxy.ai/resources/blog/10-electrical-service-kpis-every-contractor-should-track-daily-d1-36); [FieldForceTracker FTFR/parts](https://www.fieldforcetracker.com/improve-first-time-fix-rate-parts-inventory/); [FieldServiceSoftware.io FTFR](https://fieldservicesoftware.io/glossary/first-time-fix-rate-ftfr/)
- **Fix time versus find time.** [ANECDOTE] Forum electricians say the actual repair may take ~10 minutes but finding the problem takes much longer, that most electricians are installers rather than troubleshooters, and that formal training/years of experience do not guarantee troubleshooting skill. — [Mike Holt Forum excerpts via search](https://forums.mikeholt.com/threads/troubleshooting-time-allowed.3241/post-278082) (403 on direct fetch; snippet only)
- **Intermittent faults are called "one of the hardest to find."** [ANECDOTE, UK forum] An example is an RCD tripping 3x/week one month and once a month the next. — [ElectriciansForums, Intermittent Fault Headache](https://www.electriciansforums.net/threads/intermittent-fault-headache.11966/) (snippet only; UK context)
- **Practitioner-described hardest ground/neutral faults.** [ANECDOTE, snippet-only] ElectricianTalk threads list crossed neutrals (shared neutrals back to the panel), bare grounds touching device screws, screws driven into cable, nicked conductors, 1950s cloth-insulated wiring; common method is to work from the panel outward disconnecting downstream loads (divide and conquer). — [ElectricianTalk, Troubleshooting ground faults](https://www.electriciantalk.com/threads/troubleshooting-ground-faults.278418/); [ElectricianTalk, tripped breaker mystery](https://www.electriciantalk.com/threads/solving-a-tripped-breaker-mystery.293208/)
- **Concrete time example.** [ANECDOTE, single case] A practitioner (Mike Sokol, RV/electrical safety educator, May 2023) described a ground-fault hunt in a ~100,000 sq ft factory with 12 packaging lines where conventional isolation would have meant hours of downtime per section; using a pulsing 10 A tracer current and a clamp meter with repeated halving of the search area, he reports locating it in under 2 hours versus "days/weeks" predicted. — [RV Electricity substack, Troubleshooting: Divide and Conquer](https://rvelectricity.substack.com/p/troubleshooting-divide-and-conquer)
- **"Hardest part is the symptom description / operator is often wrong about location."** [ANECDOTE] Mike Holt Forum participants say the hardest part is getting a good description of the symptom and that the customer's assumed location is often wrong. — [Mike Holt Forum, Troubleshooting Resi/Commercial Problems](https://forums.mikeholt.com/goto/post?id=1930223) (snippet only)
- **Troubleshooting is the hardest skill to acquire.** [ANECDOTE] Same forum family: troubleshooting described as the hardest and most valuable skill; some people jump around instead of dividing the system systematically. — [Mike Holt Forum, Most profitable electrical skill](https://forums.mikeholt.com/threads/most-profitable-electrical-skill.37290/page-2) (snippet only)
- **Maintenance reactive share (manufacturing, adjacent).** [VENDOR survey, manufacturing not buildings] Fluke/Censuswide (600 respondents; US/UK/Germany; food and beverage, oil and gas, life sciences, automotive; May 2026): reactive maintenance flat at 36%, predictive up from 9% to 18%, ~78% of reported obstacles are skills-related. Not a commercial-building sample. — [Fluke press room](https://pressroom.fluke.com/fluke-survey-finds-predictive-maintenance-adoption-doubles-as-manufacturers-boost-digital-investment/)
- **Insulation testing mostly used only reactively.** [SECONDARY, IEEE paper by ABB-affiliated authors, so vendor-adjacent] The IEEE ESW 2023 paper on NFPA 70B says insulation resistance testing is "often only used to troubleshoot a motor that has tripped overloads or circuit breakers repeatedly," and that a large percentage of failures and arc-flash incidents result from insulation failure that more frequent testing could detect; it also says 70B's low-voltage breaker test requirements have been "overwhelming to most facilities." — [IEEE ESW2023-18, Valdes & Cunningham](https://electricalsafetyworkshop.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/255/ESW-2023-18.pdf)

### Inferences
- Electrical symptoms with trial-and-error character: intermittent faults, ground faults/nuisance trips (isolation by disconnecting loads), neutral problems, unidentified circuits. These are exactly "localize within a tree" problems, structurally similar to HVAC's symptom -> branch search, but the search tree is the *circuit hierarchy* and unknown branch contents, not equipment sequences.
- Repeat-visit pain is probably real but unmeasured in public data; if the target user wants evidence, it must come from a customer's CMMS/work-order logs (as the user already did for HVAC work orders), not literature.

### Gaps
- No electrical-specific first-time-fix, callback, or truck-roll data from ServiceTitan/ECA/NECA-type sources found (search quota ended before further attempts).
- r/electricians threads could not be retrieved; Mike Holt/ElectricianTalk content limited to search excerpts.
- No time-and-motion study of electrical troubleshooting found.

## 3. Labor shortage and skill-gap data, aging workforce, tribal knowledge

### Takeaway
Electrician demand is growing (BLS: 821,000 jobs, +9% 2025-35, ~72,700 openings/yr) and the workforce is aging, but the headline "shortage" numbers are mostly association/vendor claims with unclear origin. The diagnostic angle is supported by qualitative evidence that retiring technicians take undocumented knowledge with them, though the quantified claims come from CMMS vendors.

### Cited Findings
- **BLS (primary).** [HARD] Electricians: 821,000 jobs (2025), 9% growth 2025-35 ("much faster than average"), about 72,700 openings per year, median pay $63,190 (2025); typical entry is a 4-5 year apprenticeship; role includes troubleshooting with testing devices. BLS page gives no age data. A search summary quoted 81,000 openings, which conflicts with the 72,700 on the page I opened; use the page figure. — [BLS OOH: Electricians](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/construction-and-extraction/electricians.htm)
- **Age of the electrician workforce.** [SECONDARY] JLL report as reported by Fortune (Apr 2026): 39% of electricians were 45 or older (May 2023); 39% of US facilities managers are above 55 versus 28% across occupations; retirement-to-replacement ratio in skilled trades 5:2; ~600,000 jobs posted for major skilled trades last year vs ~150,000 new apprenticeship entrants; electrician employment growth cited 9.5% through 2034; 2.1M skilled-trade positions could go unfilled by 2030 and up to $1T/yr in losses (a projection). — [Fortune, Apr 21 2026](https://fortune.com/2026/04/21/america-silent-army-jll-report-skilled-trades-job-shortage-cost/)
- **NECA-attributed numbers.** [SNIPPET-ONLY/UNVERIFIED] "7,000 new electricians join each year but 10,000 retire," ">80,000 shortage," ">800,000 needed over 10 years," "nearly one-fifth age 55+" appear in Indeed/blog summaries; NECA's Feb 2026 press release I opened contains no numbers (only qualitative "unprecedented demand"). — [Indeed career advice](https://www.indeed.com/career-advice/news/electrician-labor-shortage); [NECA press release, Feb 4 2026](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/national-electrical-contractors-association-neca-growing-need-for-skilled-workers-to-join-the-electrical-trade-302679393.html)
- **Hiring difficulty.** [SNIPPET-ONLY] A search summary says 77% of construction firms say electricians are hard to hire and 57% of firms say applicants lack required skills (source likely a construction-industry workforce survey; not opened). — [Electrical Contractor magazine search result](https://ecmag.com/section/your-business/skilled-worker-shortages-reignite-concerns-and-motivate-neca-action)
- **Tribal knowledge in facilities.** [VENDOR, CMMS blogs, treat skeptically] Claims such as "73% of facilities report no formal knowledge transfer process," "42% of maintenance technicians over 55," and "18-month gap between a senior retirement and replacement productivity" come from CMMS vendors with no traceable primary survey in the excerpts. — [OxMaint facility workforce 2026](https://oxmaint.com/industries/facility-management/facility-management-workforce-skills-gap-2026); [Coast skilled labor](https://coastapp.com/blog/skilled-labor-shortage-maintenance/). A Facility Executive article on lost institutional knowledge exists but returned 403, so its data is unchecked. — [Facility Executive](https://facilityexecutive.com/maintenance-teams-face-growing-risk-of-lost-institutional-knowledge/)
- **School-facility condition (institutional context).** [HARD] GAO-20-494 (2020, survey of districts plus 55 school site visits in 2019): ~54% of districts need to update or replace multiple building systems; 41% need HVAC updates in at least half their schools (~36,000 schools). Electrical not broken out in the excerpt. — [GAO-20-494](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-20-494)

### Inferences
- The shortage is real at the directional level (BLS growth, JLL ratios), but exact shortage counts should be cited as "association estimates." The more decision-relevant claim for a diagnostic tool is junior-heavy staffing + senior retirements, which echoes the user's earlier finding that a veteran thought the HVAC tool helps less-experienced engineers most.
- Public-sector/school/hospital facilities combine aging infrastructure (GAO) with aging staff (39% of FMs >55), which is an adoption driver, but also lower budgets.

### Gaps
- ABC and AGC workforce numbers were not retrieved (search quota ended); IEC data not found.
- No electrical-specific tribal-knowledge study from a non-vendor source.
- Nothing found on the share of commercial electrical *service* technicians (vs. new-construction installers) in the workforce, which matters because the tool's users are service/maintenance electricians.

## 4. Documentation problem: panel schedules, one-lines, circuit directories

### Takeaway
Everyone in the trade says electrical documentation is often missing, stale or wrong, and standards (NEC, NFPA 70B-2023, NFPA 70E, OSHA) require it to be accurate, but I found no rigorous survey that quantifies how often. All prevalence claims I found are qualitative or vendor-sourced.

### Cited Findings
- **Qualitative statements of prevalence.** [VENDOR/ANECDOTE] ResQ (maker of an AI assistant for electricians) calls documentation gaps "one of the most persistent problems in field service" and says as-builts "go stale the moment someone makes a change"; the page gives no statistics. — [ResQ/Nora blog](https://www.getresq.com/nora/nora-blog/understanding-electrical-diagrams). Arc-flash consultants say outdated or incomplete one-lines are among the most common causes of inaccurate arc-flash studies and that in many facilities the single-line "is outdated, incomplete, or does not exist." [VENDOR] — [Arc Flash Pro](https://www.arcflashpro.com/arc-flash-study-assessment-hidden-hazards/); [Electrical Reliability Services](https://www.electricalreliabilityservices.com/en-emea/resource-center/blog/arc-flash-study-risks-understanding-what-can-invalidate-your-assessment/) (search-result summaries)
- **Arc-flash label compliance.** [ANECDOTE/SECONDARY] An Aug 2026 EC&M piece (Randy Barnett, CESCP) says many facilities have noncompliant labels and skip the required five-year incident-energy review, but provides no data. — [EC&M, Arc Flash Labeling](https://www.ecmweb.com/safety/article/55395165/arc-flash-labeling-why-many-facilities-are-out-of-compliance). A "65% of facilities fail initial compliance audits" figure surfaced in a search summary without clear provenance. [UNVERIFIED]
- **Joint Commission breaker-schedule accuracy.** [SNIPPET-ONLY, low confidence] Hospital surveyors reportedly score "electrical breaker schedule accuracy" under EC.02.05.01 labeling (about 61% citation rate per a vendor summary). This would be the closest quantitative proxy for panel-directory inaccuracy, but needs a primary Joint Commission source. — [OxMaint Joint Commission guide](https://oxmaint.com/industries/healthcare/joint-commission-maintenance-compliance-guide)
- **Standards requirements (what "should" exist).** [HARD/SECONDARY] NFPA 70B-2023 Chapter 6 requires obtaining, documenting and storing electrical-system knowledge, including single-line diagrams, short-circuit/coordination/arc-flash studies that must be reviewed when the system changes; 70B became a standard (not just a recommended practice) in the 2023 edition, with a condition-of-maintenance concept (equipment conditions 1-3) and label-on-equipment. — [IEEE ESW2023-18](https://electricalsafetyworkshop.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/255/ESW-2023-18.pdf); [Daily Reporter on 70B-2023](https://dailyreporter.com/2024/09/25/nfpa-70b-2023-standard-for-electrical-equipment-maintenance/) (search result). The 70B scope covers industrial and commercial, not residential.
- **Forum evidence of bad directories.** [ANECDOTE] Mike Holt Forum posts about mislabeled circuits and panel directories filled in inaccurately by earlier electricians, requiring re-identification before work. — [Mike Holt Forum, Another Mislabeled Circuit](https://forums.mikeholt.com/threads/another-mislabeled-circuit.2573566/post-2826370); [Mike Holt Forum, Panel upside down?](https://forums.mikeholt.com/threads/panel-upside-down.56150/post-915135) (snippets only)
- **Digitization vendors.** [VENDOR] ResQ's Nora, Cruxi, ERS and other as-built/one-line drafting services market solutions; none provided independent accuracy rates in the pages I saw. — [Cruxi as-built drawings](https://cruxi.ai/pages/electrical-safety-bids/as-built-electrical-drawings.html); [ERS single-line diagrams](https://www.electricalreliabilityservices.com/en-us/services/single-line-diagrams/)
- **Study of drawings vs building (cross-domain).** No electrical-specific study found. (The user's own `doc_mismatch` benchmark is the nearest relevant artifact.)

### Inferences
- Because circuit directories and one-lines are an unverified map, an electrical diagnostic tool cannot assume "the relevant subsystem map" is correct. Verifying/repairing the map (does breaker 14 really feed this room?) is itself a diagnostic task, and it matches the user's prior documentation-mismatch/active-testing work, so a documentation-trust layer (tests that confirm or falsify a map edge) is arguably where the user's existing machinery transfers best.
- A sourced rate of "X% of panel schedules are wrong" would be valuable for a pitch but does not exist in anything I found; collecting it (e.g., sampling panels during a pilot) could be a publishable contribution.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed or association survey on panel-schedule/one-line accuracy; no Schneider/Eaton/ABB survey with a hard figure found (searched specifically; results returned qualitative statements only).
- Primary Joint Commission citation table not obtained.
- No data on how much time tracing/identifying circuits costs per job.

## 5. Instrumentation/data in a typical commercial building (and continuous vs periodic)

### Takeaway
Electrical distribution is mostly *inspected periodically and unmonitored*: thermography is typically an annual-ish walkthrough, panel schedules are static, and BAS covers HVAC/lighting far more than distribution. Where continuous data exists it is whole-building or by-load-category metering (code-driven), or in mission-critical sites (data centers, some hospitals) with EPMS, so a BAS-point-data approach like the HVAC tool's does not carry over to typical buildings.

### Cited Findings
- **Energy-code metering.** [SECONDARY] ASHRAE 90.1-2022 Section 8.4.3 requires electricity to be monitored by load type (total building, HVAC, interior lighting, exterior lighting, receptacles) in new construction; a vendor page adds a 25,000 sq ft threshold, 15-minute interval and 36-month retention, and 10,000 sq ft tenant submetering (threshold/interval details are from an unopened vendor snippet). This is coarse (not circuit-level) and energy-oriented, not fault-oriented. — [Utilivisor on ASHRAE 90.1](https://www.utilivisor.com/news/ashrae-90-1.html); [Socomec compliance page (search result)](https://www.socomec.us/en-us/solutions/applications/how-comply-submetering-requirements-iecc-ashrae-901-energy-codes)
- **Prevalence of BAS controls in the stock.** [SNIPPET-ONLY, uncertain] EIA CBECS 2018 floorspace counts for BAS controlling heating/cooling (40,462 million sq ft, relative standard error 43%) and BAS controlling lighting (16,645 million sq ft, RSE 52%); a summary says ~17% of buildings had functional occupancy sensing. These are noisy, so treat as order-of-magnitude. I did not retrieve the denominator (total floorspace), so I cannot give a percentage. — [EIA CBECS 2018 building characteristics](https://www.eia.gov/consumption/commercial/data/2018/bc/html/b1.php)
- **Branch-circuit monitoring exists but is optional.** [VENDOR] ABB, Eaton, Siemens, Senva and others sell branch-circuit meters/lighting panelboards that report current, voltage, power, energy per circuit to a BMS over Modbus RTU/TCP or BACnet MS/TP/IP; marketing is energy-code and tenant-billing focused, not fault detection. No adoption statistics found. — [ABB ReliaGear branch circuit monitoring](https://electrification.us.abb.com/products/panelboards/reliagear-lighting-panels-branch-circuit-monitoring); [Eaton Power Xpert Branch Meter](https://www.eaton.com/us/en-us/catalog/low-voltage-power-distribution-controls-systems/power-xpert-branch-meter.html); [Siemens SEM3](https://www.siemens.com/en-us/products/low-voltage/sem3-branch-circuit-meters-accessories/)
- **Periodic thermography vs continuous thermal monitoring.** [SECONDARY/VENDOR] Insurers (AIG, 2022) recommend regular IR surveys under load as the core of a preventive program; FM Global is reported to recommend continuous thermal monitoring for switchgear (24x7 risk reduction; search snippet); Schneider offers a continuous-thermal-monitoring option in its Power-Zone switchgear catalog. Market-research adoption percentages (e.g., "39% of MV switchgear has basic thermal monitoring") come from paid market-report blurbs and are not reliable. — [AIG](https://www.aig.com/content/dam/aig/america-canada/us/documents/business/risk-engineering/insights/thermography-aig.pdf); [FM Global electrical testing standard (search result)](https://www.fm.com/FMAApi/data/ApprovalStandardsDownload?itemId=%7BDEC14EBF-91D1-4265-8570-0A1683AE3905%7D&isGated=false); [Schneider Power-Zone 4 catalog](https://productinfo.se.com/6037ct9901powerzone4/6037ct9901-power-zone-4-catalog/6037CT9901%20Power-Zone%204%20(bookmap)_0000769460.xml/$/ContinuousThermalMonitoring-161C99A9); [MarketsandMarkets](https://www.marketsandmarkets.com/Market-Reports/continuous-thermal-monitoring-market-1433812.html) (skeptical)
- **Inspection-driven standards.** [SECONDARY] NFPA 70B-2023 sets maintenance intervals by an equipment condition assessment (physical condition, criticality, operating environment) and explicitly allows continuous monitoring to influence intervals; it lists IR thermography for cable tray and other equipment. — [IEEE ESW2023-18](https://electricalsafetyworkshop.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/255/ESW-2023-18.pdf)
- **Data centers do monitor.** [HARD] Uptime 2026 reports "monitoring and analytics tools" lead resiliency investments, and operators are shifting spend toward automation/control systems; this is the electrical segment where continuous data is normal. — [Uptime Annual Outage Analysis 2026](https://datacenter.uptimeinstitute.com/rs/711-RIA-145/images/2026.AnnualOutageAnalysis.pdf)
- **EV chargers.** [HARD, see Q1] Networked chargers self-report faults to the vendor's back office; failures are usually communications/payment/connector, i.e., data exists but is behind OEM portals. — [arXiv 2203.16372](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.16372)
- **What technicians bring instead.** [ANECDOTE] Forum practice is clamp meter, insulation resistance tester, a tester that trips and measures GFCI/RCD trip current; i.e., diagnostic data is gathered by the technician during the visit. — [ElectricianTalk threads](https://www.electriciantalk.com/threads/troubleshooting-ground-faults.278418/) (snippet only)

### Inferences
- Unlike HVAC (where BAS trends exist for setpoints, damper positions, fan status), the equivalent for distribution faults typically does not exist: no continuous per-circuit current, no ground-fault history, no thermal data. A "data-driven" electrical diagnostic therefore depends on (a) technician-entered measurements and (b) the building's documentation, not on pre-existing telemetry, except in hospitals/data centers/new construction with EPMS or smart panels.
- Electrical devices that *do* expose data to the BAS (VFD fault codes, motor current, generator/ATS status points, UPS) are the motor-circuit/mechanical-electrical interface; these straddle HVAC and electrical and may be the natural beachhead (e.g., VFD trip, contactor failure on an AHU) because the BAS-first workflow already exists.
- Smart breakers and branch metering are growing (vendors market them, 90.1 pushes meters), so a future where fault signatures exist is plausible but is not today's typical building.

### Gaps
- No credible share of US commercial buildings with branch-circuit monitoring, smart breakers, EPMS or continuous thermal monitoring.
- CBECS denominator and share-of-floorspace not retrieved.
- Typical BAS electrical points (generator/ATS alarm, phase loss, VFD fault word) not documented in any source I opened.

## 7. Safety dimension: arc-flash/live-testing constraints (NFPA 70E, OSHA) and how they change a "test selection" tool vs HVAC

### Takeaway
Troubleshooting is the textbook exception that permits energized work, so a diagnostic tool will often be steering a human into live measurements; NFPA 70E requires de-energizing unless infeasible, a documented energized-work permit, risk assessment and PPE. This makes test *safety state* (de-energized / energized-with-PPE / not permitted) a first-class attribute of every candidate test, with accidents being low-frequency but catastrophic, unlike HVAC where the worst test is usually minutes or comfort.

### Cited Findings
- **De-energize by default; troubleshooting is an allowed exception.** [SECONDARY, compliance site; verify with NFPA 70E text] Energized work is permitted only if de-energizing creates additional/increased hazards (life support, emergency alarms, ventilation, illumination) or is infeasible due to equipment design or operational limitations, with "testing/troubleshooting that can only be done energized" a standard example; business continuity ("we can't take the load down") is not a valid justification. — [Mission Critical Safety, NFPA 70E Article 130](https://missioncriticalsafety.org/nfpa-70e/130-energized-work/)
- **Permit and risk assessment.** [SECONDARY] An energized electrical work permit documents circuit/equipment, justification, shock and arc-flash risk assessment (incident energy/category, boundaries), PPE, and (in the newest edition per this site) an additional trained person outside the boundaries when PPE is specified. The page labels the newest edition "2027" and says the permit moved from 130.2(B) to 130.3; I could not confirm edition dates against NFPA, so verify. — [Mission Critical Safety](https://missioncriticalsafety.org/nfpa-70e/130-energized-work/)
- **2024 edition tightened absence-of-voltage verification.** [SNIPPET-ONLY] Testing must occur at each point of work, not just at the upstream disconnect, and the test instrument must be rated for the available fault current at that location. — [Electrical Safety Publications on 2024 70E](https://www.electricalsafetypub.com/nfpa-70e/updates-to-the-2024-edition-of-nfpa-70e/)
- **Documentation feeds the safety case.** [VENDOR/SECONDARY] Arc-flash studies depend on accurate one-lines and protective-device settings; the 70E five-year review requirement and label content tie bad documentation directly to PPE selection errors. — [Zech Engineers, when to update arc flash study](https://www.zechengineers.com/blog/when-to-update-arc-flash-study/) (search result); [Arc Flash Pro](https://www.arcflashpro.com/arc-flash-study-assessment-hidden-hazards/)
- **Workplace electrical fatalities.** [HARD, BLS CFOI via ESFI] 1,940 workplace electrical fatalities in 2011-2023 (~150/yr; 5.6% of all fatalities); 74% were in non-electrical occupations; electricians had 212 deaths, construction laborers 131, electrical power installers 122. Causes (fetched ESFI/IAEI table): overhead power lines 42.8%, unexpected contact 19.3%, nearby energized equipment 12.7%, working on energized parts 4.1%, ground faults 4.0%, damaged wiring/equipment 3.1%. A search summary gave 48.2% for overhead lines, a conflict; use the fetched table. Non-fatal electrical injuries with days away: 3,260 in 2021-2022 combined and 5,180 in 2023-2024 combined (a reported 59% increase; 2023-24 figure from search summary). — [IAEI/ESFI workplace statistics 2011-2023](https://iaeimagazine.org/electrical-safety/workplace-injury-fatality-statistics-2011-2023/); [EC&M on ESFI](https://www.ecmweb.com/safety/article/55266340/electrical-safety-foundation-international-releases-updated-workplace-safety-statistics); [ESFI workplace stats](https://www.esfi.org/workplace-safety/workplace-injury-fatality-statistics/) (2023-24 number from search summary)
- **Arc-flash incident frequency is poorly measured; a famous number is unsourced.** [SECONDARY, reliability article summarizing a 2024 Johns Hopkins study] The widely repeated "5 to 10 arc flash explosions per day" has no traceable primary source (Fire Protection Research Foundation 2015 notes unclear origin); a Johns Hopkins 2024 analysis identified 1,291 arc-flash reports in OSHA/NIOSH databases (1,823 injuries, 277 deaths) from the early 1980s-2022 and extrapolated roughly 630 arc incidents/yr (range 350-1,050); arc flash accounted for only 2% (26 of 1,291) of electrical fatalities in the OSHA 170 data. Vendor arc-flash marketing statistics should be discounted. — [Relia Magazine arc flash statistics guide](https://reliamag.com/guides/arc-flash-statistics/)
- **Maintenance-condition link to arc flash.** [SECONDARY, ABB-affiliated authors] Improper maintenance can lead to arcing faults; 70E treats poorly maintained equipment as exposed live parts; the authors state the continuing number of arc-flash incidents suggests requirements are "not enough or not properly applied." — [IEEE ESW2023-18](https://electricalsafetyworkshop.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/255/ESW-2023-18.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Action space differs from HVAC.** In HVAC, most candidate checks are low-risk (read a trend, check a filter, measure a pressure). In electrical, candidate tests fall into classes: (1) remote/passive reads (BAS points, meter history, thermography from outside the boundary), (2) de-energized tests (insulation resistance, continuity, ring-out) requiring LOTO and outage coordination, (3) energized measurements requiring a permit and PPE. A tool must carry a safety class and required qualification per test and must never recommend class 3 without surfacing the justification/permit requirement; "cheapest test first" must be re-defined to include outage cost to occupants (hospital, school) and risk, not just minutes.
- **Value of a test = information gain divided by (time + outage + risk-exposure),** and de-energization changes the symptom itself (intermittent faults may vanish), which the NFPA-cited "diagnosing faults that vanish when de-energized" language reflects. The tool's objective function and the pre-registered "safety overrides speed" rule from the user's HVAC work generalize naturally, but the safety constraint becomes hard filtering rather than a soft penalty.
- **Liability and regulatory posture.** A test-recommendation tool in a 70E context probably needs to defer to the employer's EEWP process and qualified-person judgment; it can prioritize and sequence, but cannot approve energized work.
- **Opportunity read.** The analogy to the HVAC tool holds for *localization problems* (ground faults, nuisance trips, intermittent loads, circuit identification), where the searched structure is a tree and cheap passive evidence exists. It is weaker for *condition-based* work (thermography, 70B maintenance), where the pain is scheduling, documentation and compliance, not symptom diagnosis.
