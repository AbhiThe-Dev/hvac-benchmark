## A. Building and trusting a Bayesian diagnostic model from expert knowledge

### 1. Pathfinder: expert-elicited probabilities, explicit dependency modelling, and evaluation against the expert
- **Technique:** Elicit disease-by-finding probabilities from a domain expert, model dependencies between findings with a belief network instead of assuming conditional independence, and evaluate against the expert with decision-theoretic and informal metrics.
- **Field and source:** Lymph-node pathology. Heckerman and Nathwani, "An Evaluation of the Diagnostic Accuracy of Pathfinder," Computers and Biomedical Research 25(1):56-74, 1992 (https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/evaluation-diagnostic-accuracy-pathfinder/). Also Heckerman, "An Empirical Comparison of Three Inference Methods" (UAI 1988, https://arxiv.org/abs/1304.2357); Heckerman, Horvitz, Nathwani 1989 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2245693); Ng and Abramson, "A Sensitivity Analysis of Pathfinder" (https://arxiv.org/abs/1304.1106).
- **What it does:** Per the abstract of "Toward Normative Expert Systems Part I" (https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/toward-normative-expert-systems-part/), the team's first attempts were non-decision-theoretic and experimental and theoretical results led them back to probability and decision theory. The 1992 evaluation compared a version that wrongly assumed all findings are conditionally independent given the disease with a version that used a belief network for dependencies. Both assume the diseases are mutually exclusive and exhaustive, which the authors judged reasonable for this domain. The 1988 paper compared three inference rules using expert-rating and decision-theoretic metrics. Ng and Abramson asked how imprecise the elicited parameters can be before accuracy suffers.
- **Evidence of effect:** Qualitative only in what I read. The 1992 abstract says modelling the dependencies was cost effective and that the richer version was at least as accurate as the Pathfinder expert. The abstract reports no numbers. No quantitative evidence found in the pages I could read (the full-text PDF came back as binary).
- **What it needs:** A cooperative domain expert for months, cases with expert-confirmed diagnoses, and a decision-theoretic utility scale (not just top-1) to see differences between versions.
- **Failure modes / caveats:** The early independence assumption is the classic failure: correlated findings get double counted. Elicitation effort was the bottleneck; Ng and Abramson state that parameters take more refinement time than structure.
- **Read status:** ABSTRACT_ONLY for all four sources. A search-result summary also mentioned a reduction in the number of probabilities the expert had to assess from similarity networks and partitions (figures given were 75,000 to 14,000). I could not verify that figure and do not rely on it.
- **Relevance to us [INFERENCE]:** Checks that share a physical mechanism (for example two temperature readings on the same loop) may be correlated given the cause, and a naive-Bayes posterior would double count them. Also, comparing the tool against an expert technician on the same visits with a utility-based metric is the Pathfinder template for our eventual field test.

### 2. Noisy-OR and leaky noisy-OR to cut parameters, with sparse-data learning (QMR-DT, Hepar II)
- **Technique:** Replace each full conditional probability table with a canonical noisy-OR or noisy-MAX gate (one parameter per cause-effect link plus a "leak" for unmodelled causes), so the number of numbers to elicit or learn grows with the number of links, not with the number of cause combinations.
- **Field and source:** (a) Shwe, Middleton, Heckerman, Henrion, Horvitz, Lehmann, Cooper, "Probabilistic diagnosis using a reformulation of the INTERNIST-1/QMR knowledge base. I" and Middleton et al. "II. Evaluation of diagnostic performance," Methods Inf Med 30(4), 1991 (via https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=%22Probabilistic%20diagnosis%20using%20a%20reformulation%20of%20the%20INTERNIST-1%2FQMR%20knowledge%20base%22&format=json&resultType=core&pageSize=5). (b) Pradhan, Provan, Henrion, "Experimental analysis of large belief networks for medical diagnosis," SCAMC 1994 (same record). (c) Oniśko, Druzdzel, Wasyluk, "Learning Bayesian network parameters from small data sets: application of Noisy-OR gates," Int J Approximate Reasoning 27(2):165-182, 2001 (abstract at https://sites.pitt.edu/~druzdzel/abstracts/ijar01.html). (d) Zagorecki and Druzdzel, "Knowledge engineering for Bayesian networks: how common are noisy-MAX distributions in practice?" IEEE Trans SMC-Systems 43(1), 2013 (found via search summary only).
- **What it does:** QMR-DT turned a legacy expert knowledge base into a two-level network (diseases above, findings below) with noisy-OR links, with probabilities drawn from the knowledge-base profiles plus national discharge statistics for priors. Pradhan et al. used a generalized noisy-OR with leak probabilities for unspecified causes and found that binary simplification of the variables gave diagnostic accuracy similar to the full multi-valued network. Oniśko et al. used noisy-OR gates to learn Hepar II's parameters from a small set of real patient records where many parent combinations have few or no records.
- **Evidence of effect:** For (c), the abstract reports the noisy-OR-enhanced multiple-disorder model was 6.7% more accurate than the plain multiple-disorder model learned from the same small data, and 14.3% more accurate than a single-disorder model. The page does not say whether these are relative or absolute points, and does not give the case count. A conference version reported smaller figures (about 6% and 10%) per a search summary. For (a), the abstracts report no numeric comparison with original QMR on the test cases. For (d), the abstract says noisy-MAX fit surprisingly well for up to half of the tables in two of three real networks (Alarm, Hailfinder, Hepar II), whether expert-elicited or learned (search summary of the abstract).
- **What it needs:** A structure with cause-to-finding links that are plausibly independent mechanisms. Priors for each cause and a leak. Small data are fine because noisy-OR needs only single-link statistics.
- **Failure modes / caveats:** Noisy-OR assumes independent causal mechanisms, so interacting causes (inhibition, masking) are mis-modelled. QMR-DT's inference was approximate simulation. Hepar II's gain is from one liver-disease network, not a repeated result. None of these papers reports a field deployment.
- **Read status:** ABSTRACT_ONLY for (a), (b), (c) (author page abstract). SNIPPET for (d).
- **Relevance to us [INFERENCE]:** Our 11 two-fault pairs and the "other" cause map naturally onto noisy-OR with a leak: pair signatures can be composed from single-fault signatures instead of elicited separately, cutting parameters. The risk is two-fault interactions that are not independent (a fault that masks another's symptom), which a technician interview should probe.

### 3. How precise do elicited parameters need to be? Sensitivity of diagnostic Bayesian networks to parameter noise, overconfidence and structure errors
- **Technique:** Run a sensitivity study on a network, rounding or adding noise to parameters (and dropping nodes or arcs) to learn where elicitation effort pays off, and apply a floor so no parameter is exactly zero.
- **Field and source:** Oniśko and Druzdzel, "Impact of precision of Bayesian network parameters on accuracy of medical diagnostic systems," Artif Intell Med 57(3):197-206, 2013 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4486041). Oniśko and Druzdzel, "Sensitivity of Bayesian Networks to Noise in Their Parameters," Entropy 26(11):963, 2024 (read via https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC11592589/fullTextXML). Oniśko and Druzdzel, "Sensitivity of Bayesian Networks to Errors in Their Structure," Entropy 26(11):975, 2024 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11592709/).
- **What it does:** Using Hepar II (liver disease, 699 patient records) and six to seven UCI medical datasets, the authors treated the original network as truth and degraded it. Parameter study 2013: round probabilities to coarser grids. Parameter study 2024: add log-odds noise, either symmetric, overconfident (extremes pushed further out), or underconfident. Structure study 2024: remove nodes, remove arcs, reverse arcs.
- **Evidence of effect:**
  - 2013: Hepar II baseline is 57% top-1 (69% top-2, 75% top-3, 79% top-4, leave-one-out). Rounding hurt mainly because small probabilities became exact zeros and the system then rejected cases; once zeros were replaced by a small epsilon (0.0001 to 0.01), accuracy stayed nearly flat even at the coarsest rounding.
  - 2024 noise: small noise (sigma below 0.2) had no significant effect; accuracy fell with more noise. Reported order from least to most harmful: overconfident, then symmetric, then underconfident. Noise in laboratory-result nodes, disease nodes, and the Markov blanket of the disease nodes mattered most.
  - 2024 structure: in the Cardiotocography model, AUC for the most prevalent class fell from 0.945 to 0.833 with 20% of the important nodes removed and to 0.637 with 40% removed. Removing about 10% of edges had little effect, deterioration became evident around 30 to 40% of edges removed, and reversing arcs was the most forgiving. The authors conclude structure matters more than parameters.
- **What it needs:** A working model plus compute; no new field data. The sensitivity run itself is cheap.
- **Failure modes / caveats:** The "truth" networks were learned from data, so noise was added around data-fitted values, not around elicited guesses that may be systematically biased. Experiments did not test spurious extra arcs. Accuracy was measured on the network's own cases, so it says nothing about shift to a new population.
- **Read status:** FULL_TEXT for the 2013 and structure papers; FULL_TEXT (first 100,000 of about 120,000 characters) for the 2024 noise paper.
- **Relevance to us [INFERENCE]:** Three things line up with our simulation findings. (1) A probability floor (no zeros) is cheap protection and matches our "widened likelihoods" result. (2) A missing cause or missing mechanism (structure) is far worse than a mis-set parameter, which argues for the "other" cause and for technician interviews on what causes we left out, before refining numbers. (3) The finding that overconfident errors are less harmful than underconfident ones is a lab result on data-learned networks; I would not assume it carries over to elicited guesses.

### 4. Domain-knowledge constraints to stabilize parameter learning from very few cases
- **Technique:** Encode qualitative expert knowledge (monotonic influences such as "this fault makes this reading more likely abnormal," or order or equality constraints between parameters) as constraints on parameter estimation, so a handful of cases cannot push estimates into implausible regions.
- **Field and source:** Altendorf, Restificar, Dietterich, "Learning from Sparse Data by Exploiting Monotonicity Constraints," UAI 2005 (https://arxiv.org/abs/1207.1364). Niculescu, Mitchell, Rao, "Bayesian Network Learning with Parameter Constraints," JMLR 7:1357-1383, 2006 (https://www.jmlr.org/papers/v7/niculescu06a.html). Zhou, Fenton, Neil, Zhu, "Incorporating Expert Judgement into Bayesian Network Machine Learning," IJCAI 2013 (https://www.ijcai.org/Abstract/13/511).
- **What it does:** Treats expert statements as constraints in a constrained-optimization or constrained-MAP estimate. In Bayesian terms, this is a prior that encodes order restrictions rather than a fixed Dirichlet pseudo-count.
- **Evidence of effect:** Altendorf et al. say accuracy improves, especially with very small training sets (fewer than 10 examples), but the abstract gives no numbers. Niculescu et al. report improved cross-validated accuracy on fMRI data (not a diagnostic task) with no figures on the page. Zhou et al. abstract gives no figures. No quantitative evidence found in what I read.
- **What it needs:** Known structure and a set of defensible qualitative constraints; a constrained optimizer or sampler.
- **Failure modes / caveats:** Wrong constraints bias estimates permanently. None of these abstracts reports a medical field deployment. The evidence base I could reach for "Dirichlet or Bayesian updating from a handful of cases" in diagnosis is thin; the simple conjugate-update-plus-small-sample recalibration literature (item 9) is stronger.
- **Read status:** ABSTRACT_ONLY (arXiv, JMLR, IJCAI pages).
- **Relevance to us [INFERENCE]:** Technician interviews could yield monotonic statements that are easy to defend ("a failed recirculation pump never makes the return temperature higher than normal") and are better used as constraints than as point probabilities.

### 5. Elicited probabilities are biased, so calibrate the experts and weight them on seed questions
- **Technique:** Measure experts' calibration on questions with known answers (seed questions) and aggregate their judgments with performance-based weights, and prefer eliciting quantities experts see often.
- **Field and source:** Morgan et al., "Accuracy of Practitioner Estimates of Probability of Diagnosis Before and After Testing," JAMA Intern Med 2021 (abstract via https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE%3A%22Accuracy%20of%20Practitioner%20Estimates%20of%20Probability%20of%20Diagnosis%20Before%20and%20After%20Testing%22&format=json&resultType=core&pageSize=3). Colson and Cooke, "Expert Elicitation: Using the Classical Model to Validate Experts' Judgments," Rev Environ Econ Policy, 2018 (https://www.rff.org/publications/journal-articles/expert-elicitation-using-the-classical-model-to-validate-experts-judgments/). Cooke, Marti, Mazzuchi 2019 and a reliability-engineering elicitation (found via search summary only).
- **What it does:** Morgan et al. surveyed 553 US outpatient practitioners (290 residents, 202 attendings, 61 NPs/PAs) for pretest and post-test probabilities in four scenarios. The Cooke "classical model" scores each expert on seed questions for statistical accuracy and informativeness and then weights them.
- **Evidence of effect:** Morgan: practitioners overestimated pretest probability in all four scenarios. After a positive test they estimated 95% for pneumonia after positive chest radiography (evidence range 46 to 65%), 50% for breast cancer after positive mammography (3 to 9%), 70% for cardiac ischemia after positive stress test (2 to 11%), 80% for UTI after positive urine culture (0 to 8.3%). The estimated likelihood ratio for a positive exercise stress test was a median of 21.0 versus an evidence range of 2.0 to 2.7. Colson and Cooke: across 33 applications from 2006 to March 2015, less than one-third of individual experts were statistically accurate, and performance-weighted combinations were both more accurate and more informative than equal weighting. A 2019 abstract (search summary only) reports a 58% improvement from performance-weighted medians versus 29% for equal-weighted medians in prediction error, but I did not confirm whether those figures are out-of-sample. A reliability study (search summary only) reported 90% intervals covering the truth only 36% of the time on average.
- **What it needs:** Several experts, a set of seed questions with known answers (the number needed was not stated in what I read), and time to calibrate. Seed items must be close enough to the real questions to transfer.
- **Failure modes / caveats:** A 2020 shrinkage-weights paper found cross-validated gains inconclusive, and calibration tests with few seed questions have low power (search summaries). Experts' errors may be correlated.
- **Read status:** ABSTRACT_ONLY for Morgan and Colson and Cooke; SNIPPET for the 2019 and reliability figures.
- **Relevance to us [INFERENCE]:** Expect technician estimates of cause frequency and of "how often this check reads abnormal when the cause is present" to be too extreme and too high. Seeded-fault visits or past closed tickets with known answers could serve as seed questions to weight or shrink individual technicians. This directly targets our most valuable unknown (cause frequencies).

### 6. Reality check: how knowledge-built diagnostic systems fared on hard real or realistic cases
- **Technique:** Independent head-to-head testing of expert-built systems on cases they were not tuned for, scored for correct, relevant and complete differential lists.
- **Field and source:** Berner et al., "Performance of four computer-based diagnostic systems," NEJM 1994 (via https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE%3A%22Performance%20of%20four%20computer-based%20diagnostic%20systems%22&format=json&resultType=core&pageSize=3). Bond et al., "Differential diagnosis generators: an evaluation of currently available computer programs," J Gen Intern Med 2012 (via https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE%3A%22Differential%20diagnosis%20generators%3A%20an%20evaluation%20of%20currently%20available%20computer%20programs%22&format=json&resultType=core&pageSize=3). Semigran et al., "Evaluation of symptom checkers for self diagnosis and triage," BMJ 2015 (via https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE%3A%22Evaluation%20of%20symptom%20checkers%20for%20self%20diagnosis%20and%20triage%22&format=json&resultType=core&pageSize=3).
- **What it does:** Berner tested DXplain, Iliad, Meditel and QMR on 105 diagnostically challenging case summaries from real patients written by 10 clinicians. Bond tested Isabel, DxPlain, Diagnosis Pro and PEPID on 20 cases. Semigran tested 23 symptom checkers on 45 vignettes.
- **Evidence of effect:** Berner: the proportion of correct diagnoses ranged from 0.52 to 0.71 across programs and cases; mean proportion relevant 0.19 to 0.37; fewer than half of the experts' reasonable diagnoses were captured by any program; no program won on every measure. Bond: on a 0-to-5 scale, Isabel and DxPlain 3.45, Diagnosis Pro 2.65, PEPID 1.70. Semigran: correct diagnosis listed first in 34% of evaluations (95% CI 31 to 37%) and in the top 20 in 58% (55 to 62%); appropriate triage in 57% (52 to 61%), with 80% for emergent but 33% for self-care cases.
- **What it needs:** Independently authored test cases with agreed reference diagnoses and blinded scoring.
- **Failure modes / caveats:** Berner used case summaries, not live encounters; Bond's 20 cases give wide confidence intervals; Semigran used vignettes, not real users. Systems differ widely, so one number does not generalize.
- **Read status:** ABSTRACT_ONLY (Europe PMC abstract records).
- **Relevance to us [INFERENCE]:** A hand-built diagnostic system landing at roughly 50 to 70% correct on hard independent cases is consistent with our 0.51 on a broad family of plants. It argues for reporting top-k and "correct diagnosis in the list" alongside top-1, and for treating 0.76 as a matched-world ceiling, not a forecast.

## B. Recalibration under dataset shift, with small local samples

### 7. Lab-to-field drop in a deployed vendor model: the Epic Sepsis Model external validation
- **Technique:** External validation of a deployed model on local data before and during use, reporting discrimination, calibration, and alert burden at the threshold the hospital actually used.
- **Field and source:** Wong et al., "External Validation of a Widely Implemented Proprietary Sepsis Prediction Model in Hospitalized Patients," JAMA Intern Med 2021;181(8):1065-1070 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8218233).
- **What it does:** Retrospective validation at Michigan Medicine of the Epic Sepsis Model (ESM) on 27,697 patients and 38,455 hospitalizations (7% with sepsis), scoring at the hospitalization level and at several prediction horizons.
- **Evidence of effect:** Hospitalization-level AUC 0.63 (95% CI 0.62 to 0.64) against a developer-reported 0.76 to 0.83 (internal documents) and 0.73 (conference). At a score threshold of 6 or more: sensitivity 33%, specificity 83%, PPV 12%, NPV 95%; alerts on 18% of hospitalizations; 67% of sepsis cases were not identified; number needed to evaluate was 8 at the hospitalization level (first alert only) and 42 to 109 at the time-horizon level; calibration was poor at all horizons. Time-horizon AUC was 0.72 (24 h) to 0.76 (4 h); a sensitivity analysis that counted scores up to 3 hours after onset gave 0.80.
- **What it needs:** Local data with timestamped outcomes and the model's scores; a pre-specified definition of when a prediction counts.
- **Failure modes / caveats:** The authors attribute much of the gap to how onset timing was handled (a prior study that kept post-onset scores found alerts came a median of 7 hours after the first lactate, so the alerts largely reflected sepsis clinicians already knew about). Single academic center. The paper does not recommend a specific recalibration. A search summary reports that Epic disputed the findings, arguing tuning is required before deployment and the threshold was low; I did not read Epic's statement.
- **Read status:** FULL_TEXT. A later four-system study of ESM version 2 (AUROC 0.82 to 0.92 with high institutional variability, low PPV, and a recommendation to validate locally) is SNIPPET only.
- **Relevance to us [INFERENCE]:** The same shape as our HVAC drop (0.92 assumed versus 0.69 real): vendor or developer numbers were produced under the developer's definition of the outcome and timing, which differs from field use. Our field evaluation needs a definition of "confirmed cause" and of "when the tool's answer counts" fixed in advance.

### 8. Calibration is the quantity that drifts first; monitor it and update on a trigger, not on a calendar
- **Technique:** Track calibration (observed-to-expected ratio, intercept, slope, calibration curve) separately from discrimination, and update the model when calibration drifts.
- **Field and source:** Davis, Lasko, Chen, Siew, Matheny, "Calibration drift in regression and machine learning models for acute kidney injury," JAMIA 24(6):1052-1061, 2017 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6080675). Van Calster, McLernon, van Smeden, Wynants, Steyerberg, "Calibration: the Achilles heel of predictive analytics," BMC Med 17:230, 2019 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6912996).
- **What it does:** Davis trained seven models on 2003 Veterans Affairs admissions and validated them quarterly for nine years. Van Calster is a STRATOS opinion paper that defines four calibration levels (mean, weak, moderate, strong) and recommends checking and updating.
- **Evidence of effect:** Davis: discrimination held (L1-penalized logistic regression AUC 0.76 at development, 0.75 at the end) while every model increasingly over-predicted risk; AKI incidence fell from 7.7% to about 6.2 to 6.3%; the observed-to-expected ratio tracked the event rate strongly; predictor distributions changed for 95% of predictors, and a membership classifier separating development from later data went from AUC 0.60 to 0.92. Random forest and neural network drifted less than regression models. The authors recommend recalibration when the event rate shifts while discrimination holds, full revision when case-mix or associations shift, and responsive updating rather than fixed annual schedules. Van Calster: an obstructive CAD model had AUC 0.69 at external validation but intercept -1.04 and slope 0.63 (development 31% prevalence, validation 44%); QRISK2 was well calibrated while the Framingham-based NICE model over-estimated and selected 206 per 1,000 men at a 20% threshold versus 110 for QRISK2.
- **What it needs:** A stream of confirmed outcomes large enough to estimate an intercept and slope. Van Calster suggests at least 200 events and 200 non-events for a calibration curve, and says weak calibration (intercept and slope) is defensible in smaller samples.
- **Failure modes / caveats:** Davis is one health system (VA), so it is atypical for population drift. Van Calster reports no prevalence of poor calibration. Both are about binary risk models, not multi-class cause posteriors.
- **Read status:** FULL_TEXT for both.
- **Relevance to us [INFERENCE]:** The first thing that will break in the field is probably the cause base rates and the check-reading likelihoods (calibration), not the ranking logic. With 17 causes, 200 events per class is out of reach, so weak calibration of the top-class probability (one intercept and one slope on the log-odds of the top posterior) is the realistic target.

### 9. Recalibration with small local samples: update parsimoniously, not by refitting
- **Technique:** Update an existing model with a small local sample by changing only the intercept (recalibration-in-the-large), or intercept plus one slope, rather than re-estimating all coefficients; choose a low-parameter calibration map (Platt or temperature scaling) over a flexible one (isotonic) when data are few.
- **Field and source:** Steyerberg, Borsboom, van Houwelingen, Eijkemans, Habbema, "Validation and updating of predictive logistic regression models: a study on sample size and shrinkage," Stat Med 2004 (via Europe PMC abstract record). Janssen, Moons, Kalkman, Grobbee, Vergouwe, "Updating methods improved the performance of a clinical prediction model in new patients," J Clin Epidemiol 61(1):76-86, 2008 (abstract via Europe PMC). Riley et al., "Minimum sample size for external validation of a clinical prediction model with a binary outcome," Stat Med 2021 (abstract via Europe PMC). Guo et al., "On Calibration of Modern Neural Networks," 2017 (https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.04599). scikit-learn calibration documentation (https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/calibration.html).
- **What it does:** Steyerberg used a TIMI-II model for 30-day mortality after heart attack, validated and updated on GUSTO-I with validation samples from 200 patients (14 deaths) to 10,000 (700 deaths). Janssen updated a postoperative-pain model on 752 new patients (274, 36%, with severe pain) and tested on 283 others. Platt scaling and its one-parameter variant temperature scaling fit two or one parameters; isotonic regression is non-parametric.
- **Evidence of effect:** Steyerberg: parsimonious updating methods were preferable to more extensive revisions; extensive revisions should only be attempted with relatively large validation samples plus shrinkage. Janssen: simple recalibration methods improved calibration about as much as revision methods; none improved discrimination (a search summary of a related Moons paper said the incidence differed between development and validation, 62% versus 36%, which I did not verify). Riley: in a worked example with event proportion 0.018, at least 9,835 participants (177 events) were needed to estimate calibration slope and C-statistic with target precision, and 6,443 (116 events) for net benefit at an 8% threshold; these depend entirely on the example's inputs. Guo: temperature scaling is described as surprisingly effective, with no numbers on the abstract page. The scikit-learn documentation says sigmoid is most effective for small samples and isotonic performs as well or better when there are more than about 1,000 samples.
- **What it needs:** A few hundred local cases for slope; even less for an intercept; confirmed outcomes.
- **Failure modes / caveats:** The scikit-learn threshold is documentation guidance, not a study. A shared slope cannot fix class-specific errors. A Huang et al. 2020 JAMIA tutorial showed on real inpatient data (n = 10,000) that Platt scaling did not reduce ECE for a logistic regression model while isotonic did (FULL_TEXT), so the parametric map can fail when the distortion is not sigmoid-shaped.
- **Read status:** ABSTRACT_ONLY for Steyerberg, Janssen, Riley, Guo; FULL_TEXT for the scikit-learn page and the Huang et al. tutorial (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7075534).
- **Relevance to us [INFERENCE]:** With tens to a few hundred visits, the defensible updates are a base-rate (intercept) shift and one temperature on the posterior, not refitting the likelihood tables. Per-check reliability updates should be treated as separate, shrunken, Beta-style updates toward the elicited values.

### 10. Recalibrating to local base rates, including estimating new class priors without labels
- **Technique:** (a) Keep the model's relative structure but substitute the target population's baseline event rate and risk-factor means. (b) For class-prior shift, estimate the new class frequencies by an EM procedure on unlabeled target data, then adjust posteriors.
- **Field and source:** (a) D'Agostino et al., "Validation of the Framingham coronary heart disease prediction scores," JAMA 286(2):180-187, 2001 (abstract via Europe PMC); Pennells et al., "Equalization of four cardiovascular risk algorithms after systematic recalibration: individual-participant meta-analysis of 86 prospective studies," Eur Heart J 40(7):621-631, 2019 (abstract via Europe PMC). (b) Saerens, Latinne, Decaestecker, "Adjusting the outputs of a classifier to new a priori probabilities," Neural Comput 2002 (abstract via Europe PMC); Alexandari, Kundaje, Shrikumar, "Maximum Likelihood with Bias-Corrected Calibration is Hard-To-Beat at Label Shift Adaptation," ICML 2020 (https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.06852).
- **What it does:** Framingham functions were applied to six cohorts (n = 23,424) and recalibrated with each cohort's own risk-factor prevalence and event rates. Pennells recalibrated four algorithms to 86 cohorts. The EM prior-adjustment treats a trained classifier's posterior as valid for the old class mix and iteratively re-estimates the new mix from unlabeled cases.
- **Evidence of effect:** D'Agostino: Framingham functions performed reasonably in white and black men and women, systematically over-estimated 5-year risk in Japanese American and Hispanic men and Native American women, and worked well after recalibration. Pennells: before recalibration, the Framingham score over-predicted CVD risk by 10%, SCORE by 52%, pooled cohort equations by 41%, and the Reynolds score under-predicted by 10%; after recalibration the algorithms performed similarly, the share of people 40 or older classified high-risk fell from 29 to 39% to 22 to 24% for every algorithm, and the number needed to treat with a statin to prevent one event fell from 44 to 51 to 37 to 39. Saerens: adjusted classifiers consistently outperformed the originals in accuracy when priors differed; no number in the abstract. Alexandari: EM with bias-corrected calibration outperforms two other label-shift methods on diverse datasets; the abstract states the EM method assumes calibrated posteriors.
- **What it needs:** Local base rates and (for a) risk-factor means, or (for b) unlabeled local data plus a reasonably calibrated posterior; the stable-conditional assumption p(x given class) unchanged.
- **Failure modes / caveats:** If the symptom signatures themselves differ locally, not just the class mix, base-rate recalibration cannot fix it. The EM route fails when posteriors are miscalibrated. D'Agostino and Pennells report only abstract-level results.
- **Read status:** ABSTRACT_ONLY for all four.
- **Relevance to us [INFERENCE]:** Cause frequency is the single most valuable unknown in our simulation (+0.095 top-1). These results say local base-rate replacement fixes a large fraction of cross-site miscalibration, and the EM route could estimate cause frequencies from visits where the tool recorded readings but no repair confirmed the cause. That only works if the posteriors are calibrated enough, which is what the item 7 to 9 checks would test.

## C. Conformal prediction and abstention

### 11. Conformal prediction: guaranteed coverage with a small calibration set, and what breaks with rare classes or shift
- **Technique:** Split conformal prediction with a held-out calibration set to output a set of causes that contains the true cause with at least 1 minus alpha probability, with variants for per-class coverage, covariate shift, label shift and drift.
- **Field and source:** Angelopoulos and Bates, "A Gentle Introduction to Conformal Prediction and Distribution-Free Uncertainty Quantification" (https://arxiv.org/html/2107.07511v6). Ding, Angelopoulos, Bates, Jordan, Tibshirani, "Class-Conditional Conformal Prediction with Many Classes" (https://arxiv.org/html/2306.09335). Podkopaev and Ramdas, "Distribution-free uncertainty quantification for classification under label shift," UAI 2021 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.03323). Vazquez and Facelli, "Conformal Prediction in Clinical Medical Sciences," J Healthc Inform Res 2022 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9309105).
- **What it does:** For a calibration set of size n, split conformal gives coverage at least 1 minus alpha and at most 1 minus alpha plus 1 over (n+1), on average over calibration sets. The coverage for a fixed calibration set is random with a Beta distribution, so small n means a noisy realized guarantee. Class-conditional variants calibrate per class or per cluster of similar classes. Weighted versions handle covariate shift. Podkopaev and Ramdas reweight when unlabeled target data are available.
- **Evidence of effect:** Angelopoulos and Bates: at n of about 1,000 and alpha 0.1, coverage is typically between 0.88 and 0.92; to get coverage slack of 0.1 with confidence 0.9 needs n of 22, slack 0.05 needs 102, slack 0.01 needs 2,491 (their Table 1). Ding et al.: on ImageNet, CIFAR-100, Places365 and iNaturalist, with 10 calibration examples per class the average class-conditional coverage gap was about 7.5 to 8.7 percentage points for per-class (classwise) conformal versus 2.6 to 7.0 for standard conformal (depending on dataset and score); with 75 per class the classwise gap was 2.8 to 4.9. A class with about 10 calibration examples at 90% target has a probability of about 0.107 that its coverage falls below 80%; with fewer than 9 examples at alpha 0.1 the class's set always includes it. The authors recommend classwise only when the rarest class has about 100 or more examples. Clustering similar classes beat classwise at 10 to 50 per class. Vazquez and Facelli: only 14 clinical conformal papers found, mostly isolated studies without clinician input, and mostly without numeric coverage in the review text.
- **What it needs:** A few hundred confirmed cases for marginal sets; about 100 per class for per-class guarantees; exchangeability between calibration and deployment visits.
- **Failure modes / caveats:** The guarantee is marginal, not per-visit. With shift, coverage degrades unless the shift is modelled. Rare classes get either vacuous or unreliable sets. The review found weak clinical evidence.
- **Read status:** FULL_TEXT (Angelopoulos and Bates read to character 200,000 of 230,561; Ding et al. results tables), ABSTRACT_ONLY for Podkopaev and Ramdas, FULL_TEXT for Vazquez and Facelli.
- **Relevance to us [INFERENCE]:** With 17 causes and a few hundred visits, most causes will have far fewer than 100 confirmed visits, so use marginal (or cause-group) sets with a Beta-aware margin. Grouping physically similar causes before conformalizing is the natural fix. Our tool's sets could also be tied to the "stop when confident" rule.

### 12. Selective classification and deferral to a human
- **Technique:** Let the system abstain or hand off cases whose confidence is below a threshold chosen on a validation set to control the error rate among the cases it does answer, or learn when to defer to the clinician.
- **Field and source:** Angelopoulos and Bates, Section 5.5 on selective classification (https://arxiv.org/html/2107.07511v6). Leibig, Allken, Ayhan, Berens, Wahl, "Leveraging uncertainty information from deep neural networks for disease detection," Sci Rep 2017 (read via https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC5736701/fullTextXML). Dvijotham et al., "Enhancing the reliability and accuracy of AI-enabled diagnosis via complementarity-driven deferral to clinicians" (CoDoC), Nat Med 29(7):1814-1820, 2023 (abstract via Europe PMC record, PMID 37460754).
- **What it does:** Angelopoulos and Bates pick a confidence threshold so that the selective accuracy is at least 1 minus alpha with probability 1 minus delta, using a binomial upper confidence bound on the empirical risk. Leibig referred the most uncertain diabetic-retinopathy cases to experts. CoDoC learns, from a validation set, whether to trust the AI or the clinician workflow for each case.
- **Evidence of effect:** Leibig (Kaggle DR, 35,126 training and 53,576 test images; Messidor 1,200 images as independent test): AUC rose from 0.889 with no referral to 0.898 at 10% and 0.908 at 20% referral on one task, and from 0.936 to 0.948 to 0.956 on Messidor; the NHS sensitivity and specificity targets were passed when referring 0 to 20% of the most uncertain decisions. Uncertainty was lowest on Messidor, then Kaggle, then ImageNet (out-of-distribution). CoDoC (abstract): against double reading with arbitration in a UK screening program it cut false positives by 25% at the same false-negative rate and cut clinician workload by 66%; for tuberculosis triage it cut false positives by 5 to 15% at the same false-negative rate for three of five commercial AI systems.
- **What it needs:** A validation set with outcomes (hundreds of cases) and an available expert for the deferred cases. The Angelopoulos bound assumes i.i.d. data.
- **Failure modes / caveats:** Referral gains in Leibig are modest in AUC terms; uncertainty did not clearly flag all ungradable images. CoDoC's dataset sizes were not on the page I read. All three are image-classification settings.
- **Read status:** FULL_TEXT for Angelopoulos and Bates Section 5.5 and for Leibig (first 100,000 of about 150,000 characters); ABSTRACT_ONLY for CoDoC.
- **Relevance to us [INFERENCE]:** Our "stop when confident" rule is a selective-classification rule; its threshold can be set with the Angelopoulos upper-bound recipe on the first field visits, so that "confident and wrong" is controlled at a stated rate with a stated risk of failing. Deferral in our setting means "escalate to a senior technician" or "run a lab test."

## D. Honest accuracy estimates before and after go-live

### 13. Study-design biases that inflate accuracy: spectrum, case-control selection, differential verification
- **Technique:** Design the accuracy study on a consecutive clinical population with one reference standard applied to everyone, interpreted blind to the test, and match the study setting's case mix to the deployment setting.
- **Field and source:** Lijmer et al., "Empirical evidence of design-related bias in studies of diagnostic tests," JAMA 1999 (abstract via https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE%3A%22Empirical%20evidence%20of%20design-related%20bias%20in%20studies%20of%20diagnostic%20tests%22&format=json&resultType=core&pageSize=3). Leeflang et al., "Variation of a test's sensitivity and specificity with disease prevalence," CMAJ 2013 (abstract via https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE%3A%22Variation%20of%20a%20test%27s%20sensitivity%20and%20specificity%20with%20disease%20prevalence%22&format=json&resultType=core&pageSize=3).
- **What it does:** Lijmer analyzed 184 studies of 218 diagnostic tests and estimated how much each design flaw inflates the diagnostic odds ratio. Leeflang examined 23 meta-analyses (416 studies) to see how sensitivity and specificity move with prevalence.
- **Evidence of effect:** Lijmer: only 15 of 218 evaluations (6.8%) met all 8 methodological criteria. Using a diseased group plus a separate healthy control group inflated the relative diagnostic odds ratio to 3.0 (95% CI 2.0 to 4.5); using different reference tests for positive and negative results gave 2.2 (1.5 to 3.3); reference interpretation not blind to the test, 1.3 (1.0 to 1.9). Leeflang: moving from lowest to highest prevalence within a review changed sensitivity or specificity by 0 to 40 percentage points; significant in 8 of 23 meta-analyses (35%); specificity tended to be lower at higher prevalence, with no systematic effect for sensitivity.
- **What it needs:** Consecutive enrollment, one reference standard for all, blinding, and a description of the study population.
- **Failure modes / caveats:** These are meta-epidemiological associations from 1990s test studies, not cause-ranking systems. The abstract I read did not give separate figures for partial verification.
- **Read status:** ABSTRACT_ONLY for both.
- **Relevance to us [INFERENCE]:** In our setting the "reference standard" is the repair that worked, which is applied only to the cause the technician tested or fixed (partial and differential verification) and may be influenced by the tool's suggestion (incorporation bias). Our pilot logs should record the confirmed cause for every visit regardless of whether the tool was followed, and a case mix drawn only from the partner's easy or recurring buildings will overstate accuracy.

### 14. Silent (shadow) mode before go-live
- **Technique:** Run the frozen system prospectively on live cases with its output hidden from users, compare its predictions to eventual outcomes, and fix data and pipeline faults before anyone relies on it.
- **Field and source:** "The silent trial - the bridge between bench-to-bedside clinical AI applications," Frontiers in Digital Health, 2022 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9424628/; author list not captured in what I retrieved). Morse et al., "Monitoring Approaches for a Pediatric Chronic Kidney Disease Machine Learning Model," Appl Clin Inform 13(2):431-438, 2022 (abstract via Europe PMC).
- **What it does:** The Frontiers paper presents a three-stage roadmap (exploratory development, silent trial, prospective clinical evaluation) with a worked case of an infant-hydronephrosis model. Morse ran a pediatric CKD model silently on admissions and compared it with the retrospective development data.
- **Evidence of effect:** Hydronephrosis: AUROC 0.90 on a random 20% test split of the development data fell to 0.50 in the first silent trial (523 kidneys, 150 patients; sensitivity 1.00, specificity 0.0). Causes were younger patients (35 versus 61 weeks), more right-sided obstruction (42% versus 36%), and a change in image format (processed JPEG versus unprocessed or PNG). The large fix was re-reading images in the original format; adding age and laterality gave only a minor gain. After retraining on original plus silent-trial-1 data, AUROC was 0.85 on silent trial 1 and 0.91 with covariates on silent trial 2 (711 kidneys, 202 patients). Morse: development set 4,879 admissions, silent phase 1,270 admissions; AUROC 0.76 retrospective versus 0.63 prospective (p = 0.033); 66 of 75 input variables (88%) differed significantly; a classifier could tell the two settings apart with AUROC 0.71.
- **What it needs:** Live data access, a way to log predictions silently, a pre-set performance threshold, and outcome follow-up.
- **Failure modes / caveats:** Both are single-site pediatric ML models. The Frontiers body text gives 0.92 for one silent-trial-2 variant while its abstract range tops out at 0.91. A silent trial finds drift, not whether clinicians will use the output well.
- **Read status:** FULL_TEXT for Frontiers; ABSTRACT_ONLY for Morse.
- **Relevance to us [INFERENCE]:** A technician-guided pilot can run the engine in the background of normal visits: log which check it would have asked for, its posterior at each step, and its stopping point, and compare with the technician's confirmed cause and minutes. The hydronephrosis case is a reminder that the largest "accuracy" drop can be a data-capture mismatch (for us, a reading taken differently or a tenant answer phrased differently), not a modelling flaw.

## Patterns across items

- Calibration, not ranking, is what fails first. Discrimination stayed flat while calibration drifted in Davis (AUC 0.76 to 0.75 versus steadily rising over-prediction), and the sepsis model's calibration was poor everywhere. [INFERENCE] Expect our cause-posterior probabilities to be miscalibrated in the field before our top-1 ordering collapses.
- Replace the base rate (intercept) and adjust one temperature before touching anything else. Parsimonious updating beat full revision at small n (Steyerberg, Janssen), and recalibrating four CVD algorithms with only local risk-factor profiles and incidence (which had over-predicted by 41 to 52% for two of them) made them perform similarly and shrank the high-risk group from 29 to 39% to 22 to 24% (Pennells; abstract does not report post-recalibration calibration numbers). [INFERENCE] This matches our simulation finding that cause frequencies are the most valuable input.
- Parameter precision matters little; structure and zero probabilities matter a lot (Oniśko and Druzdzel 2013, 2024). A missing cause or mechanism is the failure to guard against, which for us means the "other" cause plus interviews about causes we omitted.
- Elicited probabilities are biased toward certainty, and calibrating the experts with known-answer questions is the standard remedy (Morgan 2021, Colson and Cooke 2018). [INFERENCE] Treat technician-supplied numbers as priors with a stated, modest weight and update them with data.
- Noisy-OR with a leak and qualitative constraints are the main tools for few-parameter models from knowledge, but I found no quantitative field evidence for constraints, and a single 6.7%-versus-14.3% abstract for noisy-OR (Hepar II).
- Small-n guarantees exist but are sample-hungry for rare classes: conformal needs roughly 100 confirmed cases per class for per-class coverage (Ding et al.), while marginal coverage is workable with a few hundred cases. [INFERENCE] Group similar causes, or settle for marginal and top-class coverage.
- Retrospective and developer numbers are upper bounds. Silent-mode runs and external validations repeatedly found large drops (0.90 to 0.50, 0.76 to 0.63, ESM 0.76 to 0.83 reported versus 0.63) with identifiable, fixable data-pipeline causes; independent evaluations of knowledge-built diagnostic systems landed at roughly 50 to 70% on hard cases (Berner).
- Design flaws inflate reported accuracy (case-control style selection: relative diagnostic odds ratio 3.0; differential verification: 2.2), and prevalence alone moved sensitivity or specificity by up to 40 points. [INFERENCE] Our pilot must log the confirmed cause on all visits and describe the case mix.
