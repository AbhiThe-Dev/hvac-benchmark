# LLMs as Evidence Sources/Interpreters for Bayesian/Probabilistic Diagnostic Systems

## Is there published work using an LLM to read/interpret a sensor trend or time-series and produce a diagnostic judgment or confidence estimate, evaluated against ground truth or a purely statistical method?

### Takeaway
Yes — a small but active line of work has LLMs/VLMs read plotted sensor time-series (or algorithmically-summarized versions of them) and produce anomaly/fault judgments, and at least one hybrid industrial-FDD paper directly compares a "pure LLM" and a purely statistical baseline. But in the clearest bounded-role example (HVAC-specific), the LLM does not read live sensor streams at all — it authors detection rules/code that a separate deterministic system then executes on the sensor data, and no paper found reports formal calibration of the LLM's diagnostic confidence against ground truth.

### Cited Findings
- A DML–LLM hybrid architecture for fault detection/diagnosis in sensor-rich industrial systems reports, on the same evaluation: SPC/FDC (purely statistical) Precision 0.68 / Recall 0.52 / F1 0.59; "Pure LLM" (a full RAG pipeline, not isolated LLM reasoning) Precision 0.72 / Recall 0.78 / F1 0.75; DML–LLM Hybrid Precision 0.85 / Recall 0.81 / F1 0.83 — [Large Language Models in Sensor-Driven Control Systems (DML-LLM), PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13030379/)
- In that same paper, the LLM is not fed raw sensor time-series directly; the LLM is invoked only for "semantic interpretation of unstructured information" (technician notes, maintenance reports, retrieved domain documents) under controlled prompts, and its raw output is passed through a deterministic normalization function Rn = N(R, C) (R = raw LLM response, C = confidence) plus a stability constraint |D(t) − D(t−1)| < τ before being allowed to influence the causal-temporal reasoning chain — [DML-LLM, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13030379/)
- PILLM (Physics-Informed LLMs for HVAC Anomaly Detection with Autonomous Rule Generation) does not have the LLM directly analyze sensor streams either — the LLM is given physics metadata about each HVAC feature (e.g. "Zone temperature reflects indoor thermal conditions") and generates anomaly-detection rules as executable Python code, which a separate deterministic system then applies to the sensor data; the LLM's role is bounded to rule authorship and reflection/refinement on rule performance, not real-time diagnostic judgment — [Physics-Informed LLMs for HVAC Anomaly Detection, arXiv:2510.17146](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.17146v1)
- PILLM reports F1 = 0.926 (precision 0.968, recall 0.859), beating ARGOS (prior SOTA, F1 0.902), LSTM-AD (F1 0.818), and a classical AutoRegression statistical baseline (F1 0.668) — but the paper includes no purely-statistical rule-based baseline beyond AutoRegression, and reports no calibration or error-rate analysis of the LLM component in isolation — [PILLM, arXiv:2510.17146](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.17146v1)
- Vision-language models are being applied to time-series anomaly detection by converting sensor readings into plots that the VLM interprets visually rather than processing raw numerical sequences — [Harnessing Vision-Language Models for Time Series Anomaly Detection, AAAI 2026 / arXiv:2506.06836](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.06836)
- "Tiny but Trusted" (VisAnomReasoner) is a parameter-efficient VLM fine-tuned with natural-language rationales for time-series anomaly detection, built on a new benchmark (VisAnomBench) because public anomaly benchmarks provide interval labels but not natural-language rationales suitable for training grounded VLM judgments; the paper reports improvements of at least 21.23 points in precision and 23.87 points in F1 over baselines — [Tiny but Trusted, arXiv:2605.30344](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.30344)
- TimeVista proposes VLMs as "judges" of time-series forecasts, reasoning at a micro-level (trend, phase, motif, magnitude read directly off the visualized curve) and a macro-level (whether forecasts respect domain-specific constraints) — a judgment/evaluation role rather than a diagnostic-fault role, but methodologically close to "LLM reads a plot and produces a confidence-bearing judgment" — [TimeVista, arXiv:2606.16173](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.16173v1)
- Outside industrial settings, a medical-diagnosis reliability study found current LLMs achieve 99–100% intra-model consistency and 98–99% inter-model agreement on binary diagnostic questions, yet diagnostic accuracy is only ~50% — equivalent to chance — meaning LLMs can be "reliably wrong" (consistent but not accurate) — [High Consistency, Limited Accuracy, medRxiv](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.64898/2025.12.08.25341823.full.pdf)

### Inferences
- Across the industrial-FDD papers found, the pattern is consistent: LLMs are kept away from directly interpreting raw sensor streams as an evidence-producing step and are instead used either (a) to interpret text (logs/notes) alongside a separate statistical/DL model that handles the numeric sensor data, or (b) to generate interpretable rules/code that a separate deterministic engine executes on the sensor data. This is a deliberate design choice to bound the LLM's role and keep the sensor-fidelity judgment in a non-LLM component.
- Where VLMs are used to read plotted sensor data directly (time-series anomaly detection via vision), the field is newer (2025–2026 papers, several very recent/preprint) and the emphasis is on producing natural-language rationales and benchmark construction rather than on calibrating the confidence of the diagnostic judgment itself.
- The "high consistency, low accuracy" medical finding is a useful cautionary datapoint for the Bayesian-fusion use case even though it's not industrial: a channel that is very consistent but only ~50% accurate is a channel whose reliability could be badly overestimated by mistaking consistency for correctness — exactly the failure mode a Bayesian fusion model would need to guard against via proper calibration, not just repeatability checks.

### Gaps
- No paper found reports a formal calibration curve (e.g., LLM-stated confidence vs. empirical accuracy) for an LLM/VLM reading a sensor trend/plot in an industrial or building-monitoring context specifically.
- Full-text extraction of exact accuracy/F1 numbers and baseline comparisons for the AAAI "Harnessing Vision-Language Models for Time Series Anomaly Detection" paper and for "Tiny but Trusted" could not be completed (PDF fetch tools returned binary/unparseable content and a Medium explainer returned HTTP 403); the precision/F1 gains cited above are relative to unspecified baselines per the abstract-level search snippet, not confirmed against a purely-statistical detector specifically.
- No industrial/HVAC paper was found where an LLM directly ingests a raw or lightly-summarized sensor time-series (no plot, no rule-generation intermediary) and outputs a diagnostic judgment with an explicit confidence score validated against ground truth.

## Is there work specifically on LLMs extracting structured facts from maintenance logs, technician notes, work orders, or commissioning reports in HVAC/building or industrial-maintenance contexts? What extraction accuracy is reported?

### Takeaway
Yes, this is the best-evidenced part of the research question — several 2025–2026 papers use LLMs to extract structured fields (failure mode, mechanism, symptom, cause, system code, maintenance action) from free-text maintenance logs, but none report accuracy in a standard precision/recall-against-independent-ground-truth form; instead they use audited-acceptance scores or synthetic-noise-detection rates, and explicitly caveat that these are not calibrated probabilities.

### Cited Findings
- A wind-turbine maintenance-log framework uses an LLM pipeline to extract four semantic fields from free text — failure-mode title (what happened), mechanism (how the change occurred), symptom (the reported/inferred indication), and candidate cause (possible initiating/contributing explanation) — plus system codes, six maintenance-type classes, and component-level maintenance actions, successfully restructuring over 70% of a 16,316-record dataset — [Wind Turbine Maintenance Log Labelling Framework, arXiv:2605.31281](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.31281)
- That paper does not report standard precision/recall against independent ground truth; instead a single author audited 300 randomly sampled records on a 3-point weighted scale (Accepted = 1.0, Ambiguous = 0.5, Unaccepted = 0), yielding a composite score of 0.853 (85.3%), and the authors explicitly acknowledge this "cannot establish inter-rater agreement or expert-ground-truth accuracy" and that multiple independent reviewers would be needed for a more defensible validity measure — [Wind Turbine Maintenance Log Labelling Framework, arXiv:2605.31281](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.31281)
- The same paper explicitly states its self-assigned LLM confidence tiers are "not calibrated probabilities," and that the extracted fields are deliberately left as "candidate semantic evidence for subsequent multi-source event reconstruction" — i.e., the extraction step ends before any downstream probabilistic/reliability model is applied — [Wind Turbine Maintenance Log Labelling Framework, arXiv:2605.31281](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.31281)
- "Cleaning Maintenance Logs with LLM Agents for Improved Predictive Maintenance" has an LLM agent classify each log record as accept/reject/update (single-field correction), querying fleet/service/sensor databases to inform each decision; using GPT-5, detection/correction rates on synthetic noise vary sharply by noise type: 99.3% on noise-free records, 100% on missing values, 98.7% on out-of-fleet vehicles, 83.7% on invalid categorical values, but only 27.7% on vehicle-ID misalignment and 0% correction on wrong end dates — [Cleaning Maintenance Logs with LLM Agents, arXiv:2511.05311](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.05311v1)
- The same paper reports smaller models (e.g., Nemotron-9B) perform substantially worse than GPT-5 on repair tasks, and the authors explicitly state their work is "an initial step" that does not evaluate whether the cleaned logs actually improve any downstream predictive-maintenance model (no survival analysis, no failure-prediction accuracy gain reported) — [Cleaning Maintenance Logs with LLM Agents, arXiv:2511.05311](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.05311v1)
- The DML–LLM hybrid architecture also uses the LLM to "extract meaning from technician notes and maintenance reports" as part of its interpretive layer feeding a hybrid fault-diagnosis pipeline (see hybrid F1 = 0.83 above), but the paper does not isolate extraction accuracy for the log-reading step specifically — [DML-LLM, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13030379/)
- JARVIS, an LLM-based question-answering system for sensor-driven HVAC interaction, is evaluated with 80 QA pairs authored by HVAC experts against real-world sensor data, reporting SQL-generation execution accuracy/precision/recall as automated metrics, LLM-as-judge scores above 4.0/5 on cohesiveness and truthfulness and 3.7/5 on helpfulness, plus a 22-participant user study — [JARVIS, arXiv:2507.04748](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.04748v1)
- In JARVIS, the LLM ("Expert-LLM") does not read raw sensor data directly; raw HVAC sensor data undergoes algorithmic preprocessing/summarization before any LLM involvement, and the LLM's role is bounded to generating execution instructions that a separate Agent component uses for SQL-based retrieval and Python-based data processing — [JARVIS, arXiv:2507.04748](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.04748v1)
- An industry (non-peer-reviewed) source claims 65–75% of catastrophic equipment failures show early warning signs in maintenance logs 14–28 days before sensor anomalies appear, and promotes LLM-based fusion of maintenance-log text with sensor data — this is vendor/blog content (Oxmaint), not an academic study, and no methodology or validation is given for the 65–75%/14–28-day figures — [Beyond Numeric Scores: How to Fuse Maintenance Logs and Sensor Data with LLMs, Oxmaint blog](https://oxmaint.com/blog/post/data-fusion-predictive-maintenance-sensor-logs-llm); [Industrial LLM Applications, Oxmaint blog](https://oxmaint.com/blog/post/industrial-llm-applications-maintenance-operations)

### Inferences
- The field has converged on LLMs being usable for *bounded, single-field* extraction and classification from maintenance text (failure mode, cause, symptom, accept/reject/correct a record), but every academic source found stops short of reporting extraction reliability in the precision/recall-vs-ground-truth form that a Bayesian fusion model would need to set a likelihood/trust weight — audited-acceptance scores and synthetic-noise recovery rates are being used as proxies instead.
- Reliability appears highly heterogeneous by field/noise type (0% to 100% in the cleaning-agent paper) and by model scale, which argues against treating "the LLM extraction channel" as having one fixed calibration — a Bayesian fusion design would likely need per-field or per-task calibration rather than a single trust weight for "the LLM."
- No paper in this set feeds LLM-extracted maintenance-log fields into a downstream Bayesian or other formal probabilistic model; extraction is consistently treated as a terminal/intermediate structuring step awaiting human or future work, not as a wired-up evidence channel.

### Gaps
- No precision/recall metrics against an independently labeled ground-truth set were found for the core "what equipment was touched / what fault was found / what was replaced" extraction task as specifically framed in the research objective; the closest analogues (weighted-audit score, synthetic-noise detection rate) are proxies, not that exact metric.
- No HVAC- or building-specific (as opposed to wind-turbine or general industrial) maintenance-log extraction-accuracy paper was found in this session's searches; building-domain work located (JARVIS) is about sensor-data QA, not log/work-order extraction.
- The Oxmaint industry claims about lead-time (14–28 days) are unsourced and should not be treated as validated evidence.

## Is there any work combining an LLM-derived signal with a formal Bayesian update (e.g., LLM output as a likelihood, or as one channel among several sensor channels in a fusion model)? How is the LLM's channel calibrated/trusted relative to hard sensor evidence?

### Takeaway
Formal Bayesian fusion of an LLM-derived signal with hard sensor evidence, in an industrial/HVAC/building-monitoring context, was not found in this search. The one paper structured almost exactly like the target use case (LLM interpreting text evidence alongside a statistical sensor-based model, in industrial fault diagnosis) explicitly avoids formal Bayesian combination in favor of deterministic normalization, thresholds, and stability constraints — a directly relevant negative finding.

### Cited Findings
- The DML–LLM hybrid architecture explicitly states its LLM outputs "are not used directly for decision-making" and are instead passed through a deterministic normalization function Rn = N(R, C) and a stability constraint |D(t) − D(t−1)| < τ that bounds how much the LLM signal can move the diagnosis between timesteps, with "weighting rules, thresholds, or fuzzy logic" governing integration — the paper does not employ formal Bayesian combination of the LLM channel with statistical/sensor evidence, and provides no quantitative calibration curve, no false-positive/negative rate for the LLM pathway alone, and no measurement of how often LLM outputs are downweighted or discarded ("if the output is unstable or inconsistent with domain logic, its influence is reduced or discarded" — stated qualitatively only) — [DML-LLM, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13030379/)
- A separate line of work does perform formal Bayesian-network fusion where individual model outputs are treated as evidence nodes/conditional probability distributions feeding standard Bayesian inference to produce a fused posterior belief — but this fuses predictions from *multiple LLMs* with each other (not an LLM with non-LLM sensor evidence), and is applied to financial-news sentiment analysis, not an industrial/sensor domain — [Bayesian Network Fusion of Large Language Models, arXiv:2510.26484](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.26484)
- More broadly, Bayesian fusion frameworks that treat each information source (including an LLM) as providing a likelihood function that updates a prior into a posterior are discussed in principle in the general LLM/Bayesian-optimization and causal-discovery literature (e.g., LLM-Augmented Causal Discovery uses Probabilistic Dependency Graphs where each edge carries a distribution fused via weighted averaging with LLM-supplied structure beliefs), but these are not industrial-monitoring or sensor-fusion applications — [LLM-Augmented Causal Discovery, arXiv:2608.27472](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.27472); general search synthesis, no direct fetch performed.

### Inferences
- The absence of formal Bayesian LLM-sensor fusion in industrial/building contexts, combined with the DML-LLM paper's explicit choice to use deterministic normalization/thresholding instead of Bayesian updating, suggests the field currently treats LLM output as too unreliable, or too poorly characterized statistically, to plug directly into a principled likelihood term — practitioners instead reach for heuristic gating (stability constraints, thresholds, human review flags) as a stopgap.
- Where Bayesian fusion of LLM signals has been formalized (the sentiment-analysis BN paper), it is fusing LLM-with-LLM, which is a materially easier calibration problem than LLM-with-hard-sensor-data, since all channels share similar failure modes; this doesn't establish that the same machinery would be trustworthy for LLM-vs-sensor fusion.

### Gaps
- No paper was found that reports a calibrated likelihood ratio, confidence-to-accuracy mapping, or explicit trust weight for an LLM-derived signal used as one channel in a Bayesian update alongside independent hard-sensor evidence, in any industrial, HVAC, or building-monitoring context. This is the central gap relative to the research objective and should be treated as strong evidence that this specific combination (LLM output as a calibrated Bayesian likelihood term next to sensor data) is not yet demonstrated in the published literature as of this search (September 2026).
- Full numerical results from the Bayesian Network Fusion of LLMs paper (arXiv:2510.26484) could not be extracted (PDF content was not renderable via the fetch tool used), so its own fusion-weight/calibration methodology is only partially characterized here.

## What do studies say about using LLMs as a "soft sensor" or "weak/noisy labeler" in industrial/engineering monitoring — precision/recall against ground truth, consistency across repeated queries, sensitivity to prompt phrasing?

### Takeaway
LLM-based "soft sensor" papers exist in the process-industry literature (using in-context learning to predict hard-to-measure process variables), but this session could not retrieve their quantitative accuracy figures due to fetch/access limitations; the clearest quantitative evidence on LLM reliability-as-a-channel instead comes from adjacent maintenance-log and medical-diagnosis studies showing large, task-dependent accuracy swings and a marked gap between consistency and correctness.

### Cited Findings
- Two 2025 papers propose LLM-based soft sensors for industrial process variables: one using in-context learning with domain-knowledge retrieval and explicit "uncertainty-awareness and self-explanation," claiming state-of-the-art predictive performance, robustness and flexibility; a second using a "text-based knowledge-embedded" approach for general industrial process soft-sensing tasks — but full-text access to both was blocked (403 error / oversized PDF), so specific RMSE/error metrics, calibration procedures, and any Bayesian/ensemble fusion with hard sensors could not be confirmed from primary text in this session — [A Soft Sensor Method with Uncertainty-Awareness Based on LLMs, arXiv:2501.03295](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.03295); [A Text-Based Knowledge-Embedded Soft Sensing Approach, arXiv:2501.05075](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.05075)
- In the maintenance-log-cleaning study, the same LLM (GPT-5) achieved 99.3%–100% detection on some noise types but 0%–27.7% on others (wrong end dates, vehicle-ID misalignment), and a smaller model (Nemotron-9B) underperformed substantially on repair tasks — direct evidence that "precision/recall against ground truth" for an LLM-as-labeler is highly task- and model-scale-dependent rather than a single stable number — [Cleaning Maintenance Logs with LLM Agents, arXiv:2511.05311](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.05311v1)
- A medical-diagnosis reliability study (non-industrial but methodologically on-point for the "consistency vs. accuracy" question) found 99–100% intra-model consistency and 98–99% inter-model agreement on repeated binary diagnostic queries, alongside only ~50% (chance-level) diagnostic accuracy, demonstrating that high repeat-query consistency does not imply correctness — [High Consistency, Limited Accuracy, medRxiv](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.64898/2025.12.08.25341823.full.pdf)
- General (non-industrial) weak-supervision literature treats LLMs as "labeling functions": prompting the model with multiple distinct queries per example, mapping responses to label votes, and denoising the resulting noisy labels with a system like Snorkel; this literature explicitly flags that raw LLM prompting for pseudo-labels "remains sensitive to output formatting, label consistency, and task-specific control," and that agreement across different prompts/parameter settings is used as a practical (not formally calibrated) proxy for identifying ambiguous/noisy samples — [Language Models in the Loop: Incorporating Prompting into Weak Supervision, ACM/IMS JDS](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3617130)
- The DML-LLM hybrid paper's "Pure LLM" baseline (Precision 0.72, Recall 0.78, F1 0.75) underperforms the hybrid (F1 0.83) but outperforms the purely statistical SPC/FDC baseline (F1 0.59) on the same industrial fault-detection task, offering one concrete industrial precision/recall-against-ground-truth datapoint for "LLM as a diagnostic channel" — though this baseline is a full RAG pipeline, not an isolated raw-LLM judgment, and no repeated-query consistency or prompt-sensitivity analysis is reported — [DML-LLM, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13030379/)

### Inferences
- Across every source that reports task-level breakdowns (maintenance-log cleaning, medical diagnosis), LLM reliability as an evidence-producing channel varies enormously by sub-task, and none of the industrial-context papers report a controlled prompt-phrasing-sensitivity experiment, which is a real gap given how central prompt sensitivity is to trusting an LLM channel in a fusion model.
- The "soft sensor" framing in the process-industry literature (arXiv:2501.03295, arXiv:2501.05075) is the closest published match to "LLM as one evidence channel among several," but this session was unable to verify whether these soft-sensor papers actually calibrate LLM uncertainty against ground truth or fuse it formally with hard sensor channels — this should be treated as an open, high-priority item for a follow-up fetch (e.g., via Google Scholar cache, sci-hub-style mirror, or direct PDF download) rather than assumed either way.

### Gaps
- Could not retrieve full text of the two dedicated "LLM soft sensor" papers (arXiv:2501.03295, arXiv:2501.05075) in this session (403 error on abstract page; PDF exceeded fetch tool's size limit) — their reported accuracy metrics, uncertainty-calibration methodology, and any fusion architecture remain unconfirmed and should be a priority for direct re-fetch.
- No industrial or building-monitoring paper was found that runs a controlled study of LLM output stability under prompt-phrasing variation specifically (as opposed to general NLP weak-supervision literature, which discusses the issue but not in a monitoring/diagnostic setting).

## Is there relevant work on LLMs summarizing or triaging alarm/alert floods in building management systems (BMS) or industrial control systems, and how was that triage validated?

### Takeaway
Rigorous, validated LLM alert-triage work exists mainly in cybersecurity/SOC contexts (not BMS), where validation against senior-analyst verdicts is reported; BMS/facility-management alarm-flood content found in this search was vendor/industry material without disclosed validation methodology, and no peer-reviewed BMS-specific alarm-triage accuracy study was located.

### Cited Findings
- CORTEX is a collaborative multi-LLM-agent system for "high-stakes alert triage" that "substantially reduces false positives and improves investigation quality over state-of-the-art single-agent LLMs across diverse enterprise scenarios" — but this is a Security Operations Center (SOC) / cybersecurity-alert context, not a BMS or industrial-control-system context, and specific quantitative results were not extracted in this session (search-snippet level only) — [CORTEX: Collaborative LLM Agents for High-Stakes Alert Triage, arXiv:2510.00311](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.00311v1)
- A separate autonomous security-alert-triage framework (also SOC/cybersecurity, not BMS) reports a mean triage time of 28 seconds versus 15–45 minutes for manual triage, 94.1% agreement with senior analyst verdicts, and a 95.4% weighted F1 score for severity classification — this is the clearest example found of an LLM triage system validated by direct comparison to expert human judgment, but it is outside the building/industrial-monitoring domain — [Autonomous Security Alert Triage Using LLM Based Agentic Investigation, IJSRCSEIT](https://ijsrcseit.com/home/article/view/CSEIT261213109)
- On the BMS/facility-management side, a vendor source (BrainBoxAI) discusses "AI for smarter facility management" addressing alarm overload, and general industry commentary states that virtual, LLM-based building-operations assistants will "substantially cut down on the time spent on alarm management tasks" — no validation methodology, accuracy metric, or comparison to human triage is disclosed in this material, and it should be treated as vendor marketing content rather than evidence — [Tackling alarm overload: AI for smarter facility management, BrainBoxAI](https://brainboxai.com/en/articles/tackling-alarm-overload-ai-for-smarter-facility-management)

### Inferences
- The methodological playbook used to validate SOC alert triage (agreement rate with senior-analyst verdicts, weighted F1 for severity classification, time-to-triage comparison) is directly transferable to BMS/industrial alarm-flood triage and could serve as a template if this research area is pursued for buildings, but no evidence was found that this transfer has been done and published yet.
- The gap between well-validated SOC alert-triage research and unvalidated BMS alarm-triage vendor claims mirrors the pattern seen elsewhere in this research task: rigorous LLM-as-evidence-channel evaluation exists in adjacent, more mature domains (cybersecurity, medicine) but has not yet been matched by equally rigorous published work in building/HVAC-specific monitoring.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed or preprint study was found that quantitatively validates LLM-based alarm/alert-flood triage specifically within a BMS or industrial-control-system context (as opposed to SOC/cybersecurity) against ground truth or expert judgment.
- Quantitative results for CORTEX were not extracted beyond the qualitative claim of "substantially reduces false positives" — a direct fetch of the full paper would be needed for specific precision/recall/false-positive-rate figures.
