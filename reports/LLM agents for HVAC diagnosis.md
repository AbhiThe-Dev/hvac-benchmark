# Where LLMs actually help Bayesian HVAC diagnosis

No single published paper matches the user's recollection of a GPT-4o agent that simultaneously coordinates a formal HVAC ontology, expert diagnostic rules, a queried database, and separate FDD tools to trace faults and produce maintenance recommendations; the closest documented candidate is a 2025 SINTEF paper pairing GPT-4o with a Neo4j knowledge graph and an InfluxDB time-series database at Trondheim's ZEB Laboratory, but it is paywalled and its rule-engine and discrete-FDD-tool components remain unconfirmed ([Clauß et al. 2025, Semantic Scholar](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1007/978-3-032-03098-6_9)). The broader 2023–2026 literature on LLMs in HVAC and building fault diagnosis is small, young, and converges on one structural lesson that bears directly on the user's own system: everywhere researchers have run a head-to-head comparison between an LLM choosing what to test or ask next and a formal expected-information-gain or Bayesian-experimental-design baseline, the unassisted LLM performs close to randomly, while hybrid architectures that keep the LLM as a hypothesis generator, evidence interpreter, or explainer — leaving test selection and stopping to a formal probabilistic layer — consistently win ([chest-pain RBO study, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13037032/); [ACTMED, arXiv:2510.18988](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.18988)). The doc_mismatch engine already implements exactly that kind of validated core — a categorical posterior, EIG-driven action selection, and a confidence-threshold stopping rule (`doc_mismatch/src/docmismatch/active/engine.py`), plus a deterministic per-channel trust-weighting layer built from measured channel validity (`active/trust.py`) and a Brick-ontology bridge that grounds instrumentation questions in each building's own documentation (`active/brick_bridge.py`). Measured against that baseline, most of the empirically-supported LLM roles in this literature would duplicate work the system already does well; the roles with genuine unclaimed value are narrower — extracting structured evidence from free-text technician notes and work orders the engine cannot currently read, widening the hypothesis space for equipment types outside the existing slot taxonomy, and generating technician-facing explanations from the posterior trace without touching the decision itself.

## The recalled paper exists in spirit, not in exact detail

The strongest match to "GPT-4o coordinating an ontology, diagnostic rules, a database, and FDD tools" is John Clauß, Luis Caetano, Knut Nordanger, Thomas Elvrum Lassen, and Reidar Kind's "Leveraging Generative AI and Semantic Data for Improved Operation of a Real-Life Building," presented at the first Nordic Energy Informatics Academy Conference (EIA Nordic 2025) in Stockholm and published as a Springer LNCS chapter on 2025-10-31, DOI `10.1007/978-3-032-03098-6_9` ([Semantic Scholar](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1007/978-3-032-03098-6_9); [Springer proceedings](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-3-032-03101-3)). Search-engine summaries of the (paywalled, Unpaywall-confirmed closed-access) chapter describe a framework that integrates a Neo4j knowledge graph of sensor metadata with InfluxDB time-series data, with GPT-4o processing both to detect anomalies and generate natural-language explanations and recommended actions, tested in one room of SINTEF's ZEB Laboratory ([Unpaywall record](https://api.unpaywall.org/v2/10.1007/978-3-032-03098-6_9); [Springer chapter page](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-032-03098-6_9)). That covers four of the user's five recalled elements — GPT-4o by name, a semantic graph standing in for "ontology," a queried database, and a real (not simulated) building — but the fifth element, explicit diagnostic rules feeding discretely-invoked FDD tools, is not confirmed by any source this search could read; the accessible summaries describe the LLM doing anomaly detection and explanation itself from graph-plus-timeseries context, not orchestrating a separate rule engine.

If that is not the paper, the next-closest candidate trades the ontology for expert rules: Deli Liu, Xiaoping Zhou, and Yu Li's "Large language model-driven multi-agent framework for fault detection and diagnostics of variable air volume boxes" (*Architectural Engineering and Design Management*, DOI `10.1080/17452007.2026.2647805`) uses a Planner–Executor–Reporter architecture in which the Planner explicitly "interprets expert rules into executable diagnostic tasks," evaluated on 29 real VAV boxes with "complete consistency with manually labeled ground truths" against a monolithic-LLM baseline — but its LLM backbone was not confirmed as GPT-4o and it does not describe a formal ontology ([Tandfonline](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/17452007.2026.2647805)). Neither paper is a clean five-for-five match, and a September 2026 critical review of the field — itself reporting "45 papers published in 2025 alone" on LLMs for HVAC operations — states that no paper in its corpus was classified as "ready-now for industry adoption," consistent with the possibility that the user is recalling a composite of several papers rather than one that implements all five components as separate, explicit modules ([arXiv:2609.05314](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.05314)). Two secondary candidates surfaced only through that review's own literature synthesis — an ASHRAE-rule-based "Deng et al." system reporting 89.1% accuracy with GPT-4o-plus-RAG, and a "Li et al." GPT-4o digital-twin sensor-calibration agent — could not be independently verified and should be treated as low-confidence leads pending direct citation retrieval.

## Orchestrators are replacing classifiers, but validation hasn't caught up

Most HVAC-specific LLM work published in 2023–2024 used the model as a **classifier**: Langer, Hirsch, Kern, Kohl, and Schweiger fine-tuned DistilBERT directly on HVAC time-series to output fault labels with F1 up to 99% ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-031-74741-0_4)), and a 2025 *Applied Energy* paper fine-tuned GPT-3.5 on labeled fault data to the same end ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0306261924017616)); neither involves tool use or multi-step reasoning. Genuine **orchestrator** architectures, where the LLM decides what to query next across multiple tools, are a distinctly 2025–2026 development. JARVIS pairs an "Expert-LLM" (LLaMA3.1-8B-Instruct) that plans queries with a separate Python Agent that executes them against real commercial-HVAC sensor data, achieving 92% query-execution accuracy, 90% recall, and 100% precision, versus a Text2SQL baseline that failed on 43.2% of complex SQL generations and 32.4% of null-value cases ([alphaXiv](https://www.alphaxiv.org/overview/2507.04748)). The VAV multi-agent framework goes further, explicitly separating orchestration (Planner), tool-use (Executor), and **report generation** (Reporter) into distinct agent roles within one system ([Tandfonline](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/17452007.2026.2647805)). The open-source hvac-field-service-agent project shows what a *bounded* orchestrator looks like in practice: the LLM proposes tool calls from a restricted, workflow-specific tool set, but "the server — not the model — remains responsible for argument validation, job-state rules, duplicate-call blocking, step limits, audit records, and terminal states," and its README states plainly that the system "is not a certified diagnostic product and must not replace qualified HVAC technicians" ([GitHub](https://github.com/YangLiu68/hvac-field-service-agent)). A 2026 platform paper, Buildrix, frames the whole space bluntly: existing agentic building-engineering demos are "isolated proof-of-concept demonstrations" lacking "reusable domain capabilities, human-verified evaluation cases, and standardized benchmarking infrastructure" ([arXiv:2606.25139](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.25139)) — a verdict from inside the field, not an outside critique.

A distinct **knowledge extractor / rule-generator** role sits between classifier and orchestrator: the Physics-Informed LLM for HVAC Anomaly Detection (PILLM) gives the model physics metadata about each sensor feature and has it author executable Python detection rules, which a separate deterministic system then runs on the actual sensor data — the LLM never touches raw time-series directly, and the resulting rules reach F1 0.926, beating prior state-of-the-art ARGOS (0.902), an LSTM anomaly detector (0.818), and a classical autoregression baseline (0.668) ([arXiv:2510.17146](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.17146v1)). That design choice — keep the LLM away from raw numeric sensor streams and route its contribution through a rule, a structured field, or a bounded normalization step instead — recurs across nearly every rigorous system surveyed in this research and is the single most consistent architectural pattern found across the whole literature.

## Ontology grounding is the field's most rigorously validated LLM role

The clearest, most quantified wins for LLMs in this domain come from **ontology-grounding** work on Brick point tagging. BMS-RAG combines an LLM-plus-retrieval pipeline with a "lightweight rectification layer" that enforces compliance with a predefined ontology, consistently outperforming static few-shot LLM baselines by up to 15% F1 across six real-world datasets, with several reaching near-100% accuracy ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0926580526001251)); BrickLLM instead checks LLM-generated Brick RDF graphs with SHACL shape validation rather than trusting free-form output ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2352711025000883)). The recurring design — LLM proposal plus an external, deterministic ontology-validity check — mirrors a general-domain finding that ontology-grounded retrieval (OG-RAG) increases recall of accurate facts by 55% and response correctness by 40% over four different LLMs ([arXiv:2412.15235](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.15235)), and it beats a pre-LLM statistical baseline that averaged 75% accuracy classifying point types in unseen buildings across 614 AHU sensor sets ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1474034617303464)). The one HVAC-and-Brick-specific system combining a spatial-semantic knowledge graph with LLM reasoning for control propagation, ThermoLLM, was identified but its validation methodology could not be confirmed from available text ([arXiv:2606.22911](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.22911)) — and no paper was found anywhere in this search that validates LLM-plus-Brick-graph topology reasoning (e.g., "what feeds this VAV box") against a labeled equipment-relationship benchmark.

Adjacent to ontology grounding is the **evidence-channel / log-interpreter** role: using an LLM to turn free-text maintenance logs, technician notes, or work orders into structured fields a downstream system can consume. This is the best-evidenced extraction pattern found, but every source stops short of standard precision/recall against independent ground truth. A wind-turbine maintenance-log framework restructured over 70% of a 16,316-record dataset into four semantic fields (failure mode, mechanism, symptom, cause), validated only by a single author's audited-acceptance score of 85.3% on 300 sampled records, with the authors explicitly stating this "cannot establish inter-rater agreement or expert-ground-truth accuracy" ([arXiv:2605.31281](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.31281)). A separate GPT-5 log-cleaning agent shows how uneven this reliability is in practice: 99.3–100% detection on easy noise types (missing values, out-of-fleet vehicles) but only 27.7% on vehicle-ID misalignment and 0% on wrong end dates ([arXiv:2511.05311](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.05311v1)) — evidence that "the LLM extraction channel" has no single trust weight and would need per-field calibration if wired into a Bayesian system. The most architecturally relevant single paper in this entire survey, a DML–LLM hybrid for industrial fault detection, treats the LLM strictly as a "constrained semantic interpreter" over unstructured text, passes its output through a deterministic normalization function and a stability constraint before it can move the diagnostic state, and states outright that "LLM outputs are not used directly for decision-making" — yet the resulting hybrid still cuts time-to-detection from 7.4 to 1.2 hours and raises F1 from 0.59 (pure statistical) to 0.83, with provenance completeness jumping from 18% to 96% ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13030379/)). No paper anywhere in this search — HVAC, building, or general industrial — was found to feed an LLM-derived signal into a *formal* Bayesian likelihood update; where fusion exists, it is heuristic thresholding and normalization, not Bayesian combination, and the DML-LLM authors chose that deliberately over a formal Bayesian update.

## Every head-to-head test confirms: LLMs propose, Bayesian math decides

This is the section most directly load-bearing for the user's engine, because it is the one place the literature runs the exact comparison that matters: LLM-chosen next-test versus a formal expected-information-gain baseline. In a simulated chest-pain diagnosis study, GPT-4o's step-by-step information-seeking sequence was scored against a Bayesian-network-derived, mutual-information-optimal query order using rank-biased overlap; mean overlap was **uniformly low across diagnoses** (0.06–0.10), and the authors concluded the LLM "diverged substantially from normative probabilistic models and physician practice patterns," while also over-predicting rare life-threatening causes and over-ordering imaging by more than 30% ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13037032/)). Every system in this space that performs well does the opposite of letting the LLM choose: ACTMED uses the LLM only as a "surrogate simulator" of hypothetical test outcomes, then computes expected KL divergence with a formal Bayesian Experimental Design module to pick the test — a hybrid that cut the average number of tests by roughly 50% while holding accuracy, with clinicians judging 94.5% of its choices "clinically reasonable," and whose own "implicit" (unassisted-LLM) condition defaulted to population-level, guideline-style choices rather than adapting to the individual case ([arXiv:2510.18988](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.18988)). The same division of labor appears in Active Preference Inference, where a probabilistic model built from LLM-prompted conditionals — not the LLM directly — selects the question that maximizes expected entropy reduction ([arXiv:2312.12009](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.12009)), and in MedKGI, where a distinct information-gain module handles question selection after a knowledge graph constrains what the LLM can propose, yielding a 30% dialogue-efficiency gain ([arXiv:2512.24181](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.24181)). One paper states the underlying rationale explicitly, in support of using the LLM only for candidate generation: "LLMs excel at knowledge retrieval but are weak sequential planners" ([arXiv:2606.18933](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.18933)).

A parallel line of work on **prior elicitation** finds LLM-supplied probabilities reliably beat a uniform prior and, blended with as few as three real data points via pseudocounts, can beat maximum-likelihood estimation on 30 samples — but the same paper cautions that "merely surpassing a uniform distribution does not inherently qualify these models for safety-critical applications," and that elicitation format matters enormously (asking for a full distribution in one prompt beat querying each state separately) ([arXiv:2505.15918](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.15918)). Two further findings explain *why* LLMs fail at direct sequential/probabilistic reasoning even though they can be useful as bounded inputs to one: LLMs' nominal 99% confidence intervals cover the true answer only about 65% of the time ([arXiv:2510.26995](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.26995)), and a dedicated study of LLM belief updating found that models are far more internally consistent when given explicit priors and likelihoods and walked through a step-by-step update than when asked to integrate evidence holistically, concluding flatly that "you cannot offload good statistical practice to an LLM" ([arXiv:2605.06915](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.06915v1)). Taken together, five independent research groups working in medicine, e-commerce preference elicitation, and industrial fault diagnosis converged on the same architecture without citing each other: LLM proposes or interprets, a separate formal Bayesian/EIG layer decides.

## Claims outrun evidence more in press releases than in preprints

Evaluation rigor in this literature correlates strongly with venue, and none of it reaches the scale a deployment decision would need. The largest sample sizes found in any real or near-real HVAC-agent evaluation are 29 VAV boxes in a single retrospective case study and one commercial HVAC system in JARVIS's 22-participant user study ([Tandfonline](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/17452007.2026.2647805); [alphaXiv](https://www.alphaxiv.org/overview/2507.04748)); hvac-field-service-agent's 60-case evaluation is explicitly self-labeled by its author as "intentionally small and must not be presented as production accuracy" ([GitHub](https://github.com/YangLiu68/hvac-field-service-agent)). No system found in this search reports a **multi-building** evaluation, and — more importantly for judging "does this beat current practice" — every quantified comparison found pits one LLM configuration against another (a monolithic LLM, a text-to-SQL baseline, an ablated component), never against a commercial rule-based FDD product or a human technician's diagnostic accuracy. That means published claims of agentic LLMs "outperforming" something demonstrate that agentic decomposition beats naive prompting, not that LLM agents beat established engineering practice.

The academic and industry sides of this literature also diverge sharply in how they talk about their own uncertainty. Buildrix's authors characterize the *entire existing field*, not just their own prior work, as consisting of proof-of-concept demonstrations lacking human-verified evaluation cases or standardized benchmarks ([arXiv:2606.25139](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.25139)); hvac-field-service-agent's README states deployment would require "production identity management, multi-tenant security, and significantly expanded case validation" before any real use ([GitHub](https://github.com/YangLiu68/hvac-field-service-agent)). By contrast, Siemens' press release for its Industrial Copilot for Maintenance claims "initial pilot applications" cut reactive maintenance times by 25% with no disclosed sample size, methodology, or building count ([Siemens](https://press.siemens.com/global/en/pressrelease/siemens-expands-industrial-copilot-new-generative-ai-powered-maintenance-offering)) — a pattern of confident, low-transparency industry claims sitting next to self-critical, methodologically explicit academic and open-source ones. Failure-mode reporting follows the same asymmetry: the field's guardrail-heavy designs (hvac-field-service-agent's mandatory human-approval gates for refrigerant/electrical work; the VAV framework's "verified results" language before reporting) are themselves an implicit admission that practitioners do not yet trust unconstrained LLM tool-calling for real HVAC actions, but no HVAC-specific study was found that actually *measures* hallucination rate, cost, or latency for an agentic LLM in the field — those numbers exist only in adjacent, higher-stakes domains, where hallucination rates as high as 65–88% have been reported in some specialized evaluations ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0925753525002814)).

## Fit for doc_mismatch's engine: roles worth testing versus roles to avoid

The doc_mismatch system already has a validated categorical-posterior belief, continuous-outcome EIG action selection, a confidence-threshold stopping rule, per-channel trust weighting fit from measured channel validity, and a deterministic Brick-ontology bridge that answers "does this building's own documentation already claim this instrumentation exists" per building. Judged against what the literature above actually validates — not against what would merely be interesting to try — the functional roles split cleanly:

**Roles with plausible, non-duplicative value:**
- **Knowledge extractor for free text the engine cannot currently read** — technician notes, work orders, or commissioning reports are not part of `engine.py`'s evidence today (its evidence comes from BAS-like time-series channels); the wind-turbine log-labeling pattern and DML-LLM's bounded "internal LLM" role show this can work *if* the extracted fields are narrow, normalized, and bounded before touching the posterior — never trusted as a raw signal.
- **Hypothesis-space widening for equipment outside the fixed slot taxonomy** — `data.SLOTS`/`rules.MAX_PER_OWNER_CLASS` hand-code the candidate-owner classes today; the consistent literature pattern (Zero-Shot Active Feature Acquisition, MedKGI, Meta-RCA) of using an LLM purely for broad candidate generation feeding a separate ranking layer is a genuine, low-risk extension for novel or undocumented equipment types, provided the EIG engine keeps ranking and selecting among whatever candidates appear.
- **Report generator, firewalled from the decision** — turning the posterior trace and EIG history into a technician-readable explanation (as `41_technician_walkthrough_demo.py` already stages) is exactly the "Reporter" / "external LLM" role that both the VAV multi-agent paper and DML-LLM validate as additive, precisely because it is explicitly barred from modifying the diagnosis.
- **Bounded orchestration of the human-facing workflow** (intake, dispatch, scheduling, escalation) — a role like hvac-field-service-agent's server-guardrailed orchestrator, which sits entirely outside the statistical decision and would not compete with anything the engine already does.

**Roles that are a poor or risky fit given what already works:**
- **Replacing EIG test selection with LLM judgment** — directly contradicted by the chest-pain RBO result (0.06–0.10 alignment with Bayesian-optimal) and by every hybrid system's finding that unassisted LLMs default to population-level heuristics rather than case-specific reasoning; `engine.py`'s ActivePolicy is the validated component, not the gap.
- **LLM-based Brick/ontology tagging** — `brick_bridge.py` already deterministically parses each building's actual Brick `hasPoint` edges; BMS-RAG/Brick-DICL solve a different problem (inferring tags for messy, *undocumented* point names) that doc_mismatch does not currently have, so adopting an LLM tagger here would add an unvalidated, harder-to-audit step in place of a working deterministic one.
- **LLM confidence gating the stopping rule** — FermiEval's ~65%-actual-coverage-for-99%-nominal finding and the "LLMs are not consistently Bayesian" result are direct arguments against ever letting self-reported LLM confidence substitute for or adjust the calibrated posterior threshold `tau` already governs.
- **LLM reading raw BAS sensor time-series directly as an evidence channel** — no paper in this entire search does this successfully; PILLM and DML-LLM both deliberately keep the LLM away from raw numeric streams. `trust.py`'s per-channel weighting, fit from measured validity, is already a more rigorous version of what an LLM-as-soft-sensor would only approximate, and unvalidated.
- **LLM-elicited priors as a default** — the literature's own framing ("better than uniform," explicitly "not safety-critical grade") suggests this is worth a narrow, isolated experiment for cold-start cases with little building history, but not a wholesale replacement for the taxonomy-derived priors the engine already uses.

## Conclusion

The literature does not just fail to identify a superior alternative to what doc_mismatch already does for test selection and stopping — it actively demonstrates, across independent teams in medicine, e-commerce, and industrial fault diagnosis, that direct LLM control of sequential evidence-gathering underperforms a formal Bayesian/EIG layer, and that this finding is robust enough to be treated as more than caution: it is close to a settled negative result for that specific substitution. The more interesting implication for the user's project is where the frontier actually sits: not in replacing engine.py, but in extending what evidence the engine can see and what happens to its output after the decision is made — free text it currently cannot read, equipment types its fixed taxonomy currently cannot represent, and technician-facing explanation it currently does not produce. None of those extensions has been validated at any meaningful scale for HVAC specifically; the honest state of the art is a handful of small, mostly single-building or single-system studies, an explicit self-assessment from within the field that most existing work is proof-of-concept, and zero examples anywhere of a properly Bayesian LLM-sensor fusion in an industrial or building-monitoring context — which means any of these extensions, if pursued, would be closer to a novel contribution than to adopting an established pattern off the shelf.
