# Plumbing and electrical diagnostics offer a narrow opening

**Executive summary.** As of 6 October 2026, the researchers found no public product, paper, patent or repo that combines all six elements of your tool for building plumbing or electrical: a technician-reported symptom, ranked root causes, a next-check recommendation ordered by information gain per cost, posterior updates, a stopping rule, and coupling to building data or equipment-relationship maps. That finding is real but narrow. It rests on 49 searches and about 30 page fetches, mostly of marketing pages, and absence of evidence is not proof of absence. The *method* is old prior art (Heckerman, Breese and Rommelse 1995; Dezide's patented engine), so the only defensible claim concerns the *domain, the symptom-scoped map and the data coupling*. Informal versions of "ranked causes plus the test to run next" already exist as text chatbots for electricians and plumbers (Elec-Mate, Jenova, ServiceTitan Atlas), so "nobody does AI troubleshooting for these trades" is false. The harder problem is that the evidence base that made your HVAC result credible does not exist here. Plumbing and electrical distribution are largely unmonitored, no labeled-fault dataset resembles the LBNL set, the topology you need is hidden in paper drawings, and electrical tests carry binding safety constraints. Pain is documented by consequence (floods, Legionella, fires, outages), not by time-to-diagnose; no source quantifies plumbing or electrical first-time-fix or callback rates. **Recommendation:** do not build yet. Spend the next 4-6 weeks on five cheap checks, in this order: hands-on competitor tests, a BAS point census of data you already hold, mining your 600 real work orders for plumbing and electrical causes, 6-8 expert interviews, and an HPD shared-cause study. If one trade is chosen first, the evidence favors the plant-side hot-water/recirculation complaint for plumbing and the documentation-trust layer (panel schedules) for electrical, and disfavors any recommendation of energized electrical tests.

*Confidence labels used throughout follow the researchers' scheme: **hard data** (government, peer-reviewed, standards-body or primary dataset), **hard-secondhand** (a hard number reached only through a secondary page), **vendor** (marketing or vendor-authored), **anecdote** (practitioner or forum account), and **snippet-only** (seen only in a search-result summary). Claims with weaker labels should be verified before use.*

## Pain is documented by consequence, not by time-to-diagnose

**Plumbing's pain is dominated by rare, severe events.** Insurance data put plumbing and pipe failure among the largest non-weather property-loss categories, and single events routinely cost $1M-$7M. Chubb's commercial claims breakdown (hard data, period unstated) attributes interior water damage to roofs 15%, toilets 15%, sprinklers 14%, water heaters 11%, sewer backup 5%, tanks 4% and mains 3% ([Chubb](https://www.chubb.com/us-en/businesses/resources/10-most-common-sources-of-commercial-water-damage.html)). Allianz AGCS offers a contrary framing across 470,000+ global business claims: water damage was only 6% of claims by count and 3% by value ([Allianz Commercial](https://commercial.allianz.com/news-and-insights/expert-risk-articles/water-damage.html)). The costliest public-health failure is Legionella in premise plumbing: CDC (hard data) counted 214 drinking-water outbreaks in 2015-2020, 86% biofilm-associated and 98% of the deaths Legionella-related, with 53% occurring in hospitals and long-term care ([CDC MMWR](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/73/ss/ss7301a1.htm)). The researchers' key caveat is that most flood dollars are *detect-and-isolate* problems (a burst pipe, a failed heater), not "which of six causes?" problems, which limits how much of the dollar pain a diagnostic aid can touch.

**The plumbing symptom that looks like your HVAC problem is hot water that is not hot or uneven.** Independent practitioner voices converge on it, though only as anecdote. ASPE Pipeline says recirculation loops are "typically left to operate with no visibility" and that balancing valves get moved within a couple of years with no record ([ASPE Pipeline](https://aspe.org/pipeline/pipeline-exclusive-corrosion-in-hot-water-recirculating-systems-and-velocity-effects/), anecdote from an authoritative society). A residential forum thread (anecdote) shows the trial-and-error pattern in miniature: months to trace lukewarm water to scale holding a check valve open, with the valve replaced four times ([Heating Help](https://forum.heatinghelp.com/discussion/197801/lukewarm-fluctuating-hot-water-with-recirculation-pump)). Field-monitoring literature (hard data, via a 2024 ACEEE paper) finds recirculation losses around a third of DHW energy and measured losses "much higher than design" ([ACEEE 2024](https://www.aceee.org/sites/default/files/proceedings/ssb24/pdfs/Best%20Practices%20for%20Hot%20Water%20Distribution%20Systems%20in%20Multifamily%20Buildings-A%20Comparative%20Evaluation%20of%20Balancing%20Methods.pdf)). The topology (heater, mixing valve, pump and check valve upstream of many branches) supports shared-cause reasoning, but this match is the researchers' inference, not a finding any source states.

**Electrical pain is quantified at the wrong end.** Hard data exist for outcomes: USFA estimates 7,400 nonresidential electrical-malfunction fires and $354.4M in losses in 2023, a 7% decline in fires over ten years ([USFA](https://www.usfa.fema.gov/statistics/nonresidential-fires/electrical.html)). Uptime Institute (hard data, survey-based) finds power the leading cause of impactful data-center outages at 45% in 2025, with UPS, transfer-switch and generator failures most cited ([Uptime 2026](https://datacenter.uptimeinstitute.com/rs/711-RIA-145/images/2026.AnnualOutageAnalysis.pdf)). For the everyday symptoms where technicians lose time (nuisance trips, intermittent loads, ground faults, neutral problems, flicker), no national dataset exists. The only evidence is anecdote, and much of it snippet-only because Reddit, Mike Holt and ElectricianTalk blocked fetches: forum electricians say the repair may take ten minutes but finding the fault takes far longer, that "most electricians are installers rather than troubleshooters," and that the customer's assumed fault location is often wrong ([Mike Holt Forum](https://forums.mikeholt.com/threads/troubleshooting-time-allowed.3241/post-278082), snippet-only). A single-case anecdote reports a factory ground-fault hunt solved in under two hours by repeatedly halving the search area, against "days or weeks" predicted ([RV Electricity](https://rvelectricity.substack.com/p/troubleshooting-divide-and-conquer)). Note what that practitioner pain is: *finding* the fault and *trusting the labels*, rather than choosing among tests.

**Four pain points are shared across both trades, and each is weaker than it sounds.** First, no independent plumbing or electrical first-time-fix or callback dataset exists; the ~75% figure circulating is a generic field-service number from vendor blogs ([vsight](https://vsight.io/field-service-kpis/), vendor), and one note adds that Aberdeen attributes most failed first fixes to parts, not diagnosis. Second, labor: BLS (hard data) projects 821,000 electrician jobs, +9% for 2025-35, with ~72,700 openings a year ([BLS electricians](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/construction-and-extraction/electricians.htm)), and 510,600 plumbers, +7% ([BLS plumbers](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/construction-and-extraction/plumbers-pipefitters-and-steamfitters.htm)); JLL via Fortune reports 39% of electricians were 45 or older ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/04/21/america-silent-army-jll-report-skilled-trades-job-shortage-cost/), hard-secondhand). The tribal-knowledge claims all come from CMMS vendors. Third, documentation: everyone asserts that panel schedules, one-lines, riser diagrams and valve schedules are stale, but no rigorous survey measures it for either trade; NFPA 70B-2023 requires accurate single-lines ([IEEE ESW 2023](https://electricalsafetyworkshop.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/255/ESW-2023-18.pdf), secondary, ABB-affiliated authors), and the Joint Commission breaker-schedule citation rate (~61%) is snippet-only from a vendor page. Fourth, instrumentation: ASPE describes recirculation systems as unmonitored (anecdote), and ASHRAE 514's list of what a monitored hot-water system would measure comes from a water-management software vendor ([HC Info](https://hcinfo.com/blog/monitoring-building-water-quality-to-reduce-risk-and-stay-in-legionella-compliance/), vendor).

## The algorithm ports but the evidence that made HVAC credible does not

**The decision-theoretic backbone transfers unchanged.** Heckerman and Breese's framework orders actions by probability-to-cost under single-fault, independence assumptions; exact optimal sequencing is NP-hard in general, but candidate sets at this scale are small ([Heckerman/Breese](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1302.3563); [Lín, arXiv 1304.6551](https://arxiv.org/abs/1304.6551), withdrawn after acceptance). The conditions for p/C optimality are from the researcher's background knowledge, not verified in the sources read. No application of decision-theoretic troubleshooting to buildings, plumbing or electrical was found, though this was not searched exhaustively.

**The ontologies express a "feeds" graph but stop short of the objects that matter.** The researchers read the files directly. Brick (nightly build, v1.5.0, 1,466 classes) defines `feeds` as medium-agnostic, so electricity and water fit ([Brick.ttl](https://github.com/BrickSchema/Brick/releases/download/nightly/Brick.ttl)). It covers the electrical chain through transformer, switchgear, breaker panel and circuit breaker, and the DHW chain through water heater, circulator pump, mixing valve, backflow preventer, check valve and meters, but it has no pipe, wire, circuit, fixture or panel-schedule object. ASHRAE 223P has `Pipe`, `Conductor` (one circuit each), `Faucet`, `Sink` and `FlushToilet`, but no panel or water-heater class ([223p.ttl](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/open223/defs.open223.info/main/ontologies/223p.ttl)). Project Haystack encodes the electrical tree as an `elecRef` chain ([Haystack tags](https://project-haystack.org/doc/appendix/tags), fetched; the chain guidance is snippet-only). RealEstateCore 3.x had `ElectricalCircuit` and `PlumbingFixture` classes that appear dropped when REC 4 re-based on Brick; whether REC 3.3 defined `feeds` is an unresolved conflict between a lossy fetch and Brick's own equivalence declaration ([REC repo](https://github.com/RealEstateCore/rec)). No real building with populated Brick or 223P branch-circuit or riser models was found, so a class existing says nothing about data existing.

**No open dataset gives these systems what LBNL's FDD data gave HVAC.** The closest water resources are BattLeDIM (550 MB of SCADA pressure/flow for the L-TOWN municipal network, CC BY 4.0) and LeakDB (1.2 GB, "artificially created but realistic" scenarios), both network-scale and largely simulated ([Zenodo 4017659](https://zenodo.org/records/4017659); [Zenodo 1313116](https://zenodo.org/records/1313116)). Building-scale electrical data are unlabeled: BDG2 has 3,053 meters ([Zenodo 3887306](https://zenodo.org/records/3887306)), and CLEMD has 1-second data from eight circuits over 40 days ([CLEMD](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11156870/)). Arc-fault waveform studies report 98-99% lab accuracy on seven appliances ([PMC7506653](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7506653/)). The researchers warn these figures are classification accuracies, not per-check detection rates for a technician's sequential manual steps; your own HVAC experience, where an assumed 0.92 detection rate became 0.69 against real LBNL data, is the cautionary precedent. The best symptom-labeled source is NYC HPD, which the researchers queried directly: 16.35M complaint rows to October 2026, including 2.08M plumbing, 847K electric and 2.91M heat/hot-water problems ([NYC Open Data](https://data.cityofnewyork.us/resource/ygpa-z7cr.json)). It has building, apartment and category fields but no cause field, so it can test shared-cause clustering and symptom priors but cannot validate diagnostic accuracy. The only cause-bearing source known is your own 600 real work orders.

**Four structural differences make this a constrained problem, not HVAC with new numbers.** First, safety: NFPA 70E defaults to de-energizing, permits energized troubleshooting as an exception, and restricts it to qualified persons ([IAEI](https://iaeimagazine.org/electrical-safety/electrically-safe-work-practices-nfpa-70e-basics/), hard-secondary). The electrical note's design implication is to classify every test as remote/passive, de-energized (lockout/tagout, outage cost) or energized (permit, PPE), and to treat the class as a hard filter, not a soft cost; the tool can sequence but cannot approve energized work. Your own Addendum 37 result, where safety overrode speed, previews how binding this becomes. Plumbing has its analog: raising a heater setpoint or flushing hot water interacts with scald and Legionella risk, since Legionella growth peaks near 30-35 C and falls above 50 C (snippet-only). Second, data continuity: plumbing and distribution-level electrical data mostly do not exist. CBECS numbers on BAS prevalence are snippet-only and noisy, and branch-circuit meters are sold but have no adoption statistics ([ABB](https://electrification.us.abb.com/products/panelboards/reliagear-lighting-panels-branch-circuit-monitoring), vendor). Third, hidden topology: the relevant edges live in panel schedules, riser drawings and tribal knowledge. Fourth, liability and licensing, which are plausible but entirely unsourced; no source covers insurance treatment of AI-guided electrical troubleshooting.

**The two target symptoms fit differently.** For hot water not reaching far fixtures, shared-upstream reasoning fits, but recirculation loops are cycles (so "common parent" becomes "common upstream cut-set"), causes interact, and automated outlet-level detection is weak: a 28-building study found surrogate measures (return temperature, chlorine) had only 55% sensitivity against culture ([PMC6963558](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6963558/)). The researchers' realistic first scope is plant-side only (setpoint, pump, mixing valve, return temperature), which reduces to something close to the hydronic loop Brick already models. For "breaker keeps tripping" and "lights flicker on a floor," the feeds tree is clean and matches shared-parent logic exactly, and a load-isolation step is cheap and discriminating; but detection rates have no basis, intermittent faults may be absent at check time, and the map may be wrong in the very way that matters. That last point is the circularity the electrical note flags: a diagnostic tool depends on the mapping accuracy it would need to check. A related opening is that documentation verification may be the transferable asset. The electrical note observes that your 182-case doc-mismatch benchmark and active-testing machinery apply directly to "does breaker 14 really feed this room?", and that no study of panel-schedule accuracy exists, so sampling panels in a pilot could itself be a publishable contribution.

## The defensible claim is about domain and data coupling, and its confidence is moderate

**The exact wording the evidence supports.** The whitespace researcher proposed: "We found no existing product, paper or patent (as of Oct 2026, public sources) that combines cost- and information-gain-ordered next-check recommendation with posterior updating and stopping, scoped to a single technician-reported symptom and driven by building data and equipment-relationship maps, for building plumbing or electrical systems. The method family is established (decision-theoretic troubleshooting; Dezide) and chat-based plumbing/electrical assistants exist; our claim is about the domain, the symptom-scoped map use and the data coupling." This report endorses that wording with two edits. First, drop "cost-ordered" as a differentiator in isolation, because Elec-Mate claims to start with "the simplest, quickest tests," which is informal cost ordering. What the notes found no trace of is *quantified* cost-and-information-gain ranking with explicit posteriors and a stopping rule coupled to site data. Second, add "as far as public marketing pages and abstracts show," because every competitor classification rests on that.

**Confidence is moderate, and the claim is stronger in some places than others.** It is strongest where primary sources were read: the arXiv paper (abstract and HTML), Dezide's technology and customer pages, the Harris patent, and ServiceTitan's help pages, none of which shows the full combination. It is weakest for products judged from thin marketing pages (ElectriciansMate, PlumbWise, ECI GlobalEdge, TradePals' plumbing page) and anything private. The researchers rank residual risk as follows. **High:** stealth or seed-stage startups (YC and Techstars checked only through snippets and two cohort pages; LinkedIn, Crunchbase and PitchBook not searched directly) and incumbents' internal roadmaps (ServiceTitan, Trane, JCI, Siemens, Schneider, Fluke, Milwaukee). **Medium-high:** Dezide or Hugin-based integrators with private building-services deployments, since customer pages are marketing-selected. **Medium:** patents (18-month publication delay; no structured CPC search was run, and two cited patents, US 6,456,622 and US 7,385,716, were not analyzed for freedom to operate), and paywalled or non-English literature, especially Chinese building electrical and water work. **Medium:** very recent arXiv work, since the Marozzo and Liò paper surfaced "by luck" on one query. **Low-medium:** the many small app-store apps (PlumbWise, PlumbIQ, SparkyPal, Master Electrician), none installed.

**What was not checked, stated plainly.** No competitor app was tested hands-on, so "does not meet c" means "no evidence found." The full text of arXiv 2606.13220 was unreadable, so the meaning of "hydraulic" and whether test selection uses an information-gain proxy are unconfirmed. Dezide case studies and the TradePals PipePal page were not read. In plumbing, Nexus, Opti, Vanguard, Sensorex, Nuvolo, Fluid, Siemens, Honeywell Forge, Schneider, Xylem, A.O. Smith, Taco, Armstrong, Bell & Gossett, Jobber, FieldEdge and Workiz were not researched. In electrical, Siemens, ABB Ability, Honeywell, JCI OpenBlue, GridPoint, Veris, Leviton, Fiix, Limble, UpKeep, Maximo, Doble and Hubbell were not checked. WINT's documentation and case studies were not read, so a leak-location or recommended-action feature cannot be ruled out. Electrical-side searches stopped when the search quota ran out. No G2, Capterra or Reddit review mining was done for diagnostic features in either trade, so the "visible gap" case rests on what products do not claim, which is weaker than user complaints.

**Where the gap is most likely to close.** The whitespace researcher's judgment, which this report shares, is that the likeliest closure is an incumbent extending an existing LLM-plus-documentation product (ServiceTitan Atlas, TradePals, measureQuick) into sequenced questioning, not a startup copying the method. A second route is a follow-up to the Marozzo and Liò paper that adds building data. A third is a general LLM with a good prompt imitating (a) through (e) informally, which the Jenova pages already do. The plumbing note adds a market-structure observation: Watts' acquisitions of Josam, I-CON and Haws suggest incumbents buy controls and hardware, which makes a diagnostics layer a more plausible acquisition target than a head-on competitor. That is inference from deal patterns, not a stated strategy.

## Cheap checks should come before any build, ranked by cost and informativeness

**The ranking logic.** The researchers agree the main uncertainties are not algorithmic. They are whether competitors behave as marketed, whether plumbing and electrical data exist in real buildings, whether cause priors and detection rates can be elicited, and whether technicians feel the pain. **Which symptom to start with, if one is chosen.** For plumbing, the evidence points to the plant-side hot-water/recirculation complaint, scoped to setpoint, pump, mixing valve and return-temperature checks. It has the strongest independent practitioner convergence, a shared-parent topology, a Legionella safety multiplier that raises the stakes, and Brick coverage of the relevant equipment. Its risks are weak outlet-level detection (55% sensitivity in the cited study), cycles in the loop, and the best-instrumented customers (hospitals) already served by compliance vendors. For electrical, the evidence favors starting with the documentation-trust layer, not diagnosis. The researchers rank "breaker keeps tripping," ground faults and flicker as structurally good localization problems, but the HVAC-style cause and check catalogues have no evidence base, energized tests are binding constraints, and forum electricians describe their pain as finding faults and distrusting labels. The one electrical symptom with BAS data already present is VFD trip or contactor failure on an AHU, where the BAS-first workflow exists and the electrical-mechanical interface straddles your current product; this is an inference from the instrumentation notes, worth one interview question. The plumbing and electrical researchers did not coordinate on a starting symptom, so this ranking is a synthesis. Both agree that any "electrical diagnosis wastes X hours" pitch has no public hard backing, and that the Section 1 numbers must come from interviews and work orders, not literature.

**Design rules to adopt now, whichever route is chosen.** Never cite lab accuracies (91.67%, 98.36%) as per-check detection rates. Carry a safety class and qualification requirement on every electrical test as a hard filter, and defer to the employer's energized-work-permit process. Estimate any DHW sensor detection rate conservatively and per target. Do not claim method novelty. Treat plumbing recirculation as a cut-set problem, not a tree.

## Conclusion

The research changes the question from "is there whitespace?" to "is there evidence to fill it?" The gap in public sources is real but thin, and the sources that define it (marketing pages, abstracts, snippets) cannot support more than a moderate-confidence claim. The more consequential finding is that the asset most likely to transfer is not the diagnostic engine but the documentation-verification machinery, because both trades' maps are hidden, unverified and tied to safety (arc-flash studies, Legionella water-management plans), and no one has measured how often they are wrong. A measured error rate for panel schedules or riser diagrams would be both a validation of the problem and a publishable contribution.

The practical implication is that the two cheapest experiments, scripted competitor tests and a point census of data already held, can either confirm the opening or close it within a week, before any engineering. If they confirm it, the interviews decide which trade and symptom to enter, and the pilot partner decides whether this is a BAS-driven or a technician-entered-readings product. Those are different products. Treat everything beyond the hot-water plant-side and the panel-schedule layer as unproven until a real building's data says otherwise.
