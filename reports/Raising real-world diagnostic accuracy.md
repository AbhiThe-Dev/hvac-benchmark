# How to raise the real-world accuracy of the hot-water diagnostic: what adjacent fields do

*Synthesis of five literature reads (simulator-to-real calibration; fault diagnosis from lab to field; clinical Bayesian decision support and calibration; field-service troubleshooting learning loops; expert judgment and value of information). Full notes with per-item sources and read status: `research_notes/Adjacent fields raising real-world diagnostic accuracy/` (files A to E). Most evidence is from abstracts or open-access full text read by language-model readers; I independently re-opened two of the headline numbers (below) and both held. **No source measures our exact setting: I found no hot-water-specific study, and no published accuracy-versus-number-of-cases curve for a deployed technician-guidance tool.***

## Bottom line

1. **Nothing more we can do in simulation will raise real accuracy; field data will.** Our own simulation tests (widening, conditioning, adversarial search) all stopped at about the ceiling the simulator's information allows. Adjacent fields close the gap with real events, not better simulators.
2. **The amount of field data needed is smaller than a classifier would need, if it is spent on the right things.** Fields that succeed spend a handful to a few hundred real events on the simulator's parameters, base rates, calibration and its single worst-modelled component, not on training a classifier. A classifier from scratch needs more than 100 labelled faults per class (HVAC fault diagnosis, Sanchez-Zabala and Gomez-Acebo 2026, re-read) and, trained on 63 real sequences, scored 14 to 18% (robot digital twin, re-read).
3. **The cheapest data needs no confirmed fault at all:** readings from healthy plants on a normal day. A few days of normal operation data cut a building model's prediction error from 0.267 to 0.041 (Dowling and Zhang 2020), and unlabeled real data bought about 10 points of accuracy in a sim-to-real diagnostic (70.0% to 80.2%, re-read). Our engine's biggest fragility is that it reads absolute values.
4. **The first things the first 50 to 100 labelled visits fix are base rates, calibration and reliabilities, not top-1.** Calibration breaks before ranking does (clinical models: discrimination flat while calibration drifts).
5. **Plan for what we left out.** Removing 20% of important nodes from a medical diagnostic network cut AUC from 0.945 to 0.833, and 40% to 0.637 (Oniśko and Druzdzel); about a quarter of incidents in one year of a cloud service had a never-seen root-cause category (RCACopilot). Keep "other" alive and fix the cause vocabulary with plumbers first: in service-log studies, how the cause tags were coded moved one cause's apparent frequency from 4/62 to 40/62 and from 0.065 to 0.645.

| **Recalibrate the base rate and one temperature, then stop** | At small local n, changing only the intercept and one slope beat refitting (Steyerberg 2004; Janssen 2008); recalibrating four cardiovascular algorithms with only local risk-factor profiles and incidence made them perform similarly (Pennells 2019; abstract gives no post-recalibration numbers) | ABSTRACT_ONLY | tens to a few hundred labelled cases | clinical, prediction not diagnosis |
| **Simulator as a discounted prior with an explicit gap, borrowing dynamically** | Pooling helped only in a narrow band of similarity and inflated error to about 20% outside it; robust-mixture and hierarchical priors widen the band (Viele 2014; Schmidli 2014); simulator-as-prior with a learned gap needed under half the data (Rothfuss 2024) | FULL_TEXT | prior pseudo-count strength chosen explicitly | clinical trials and robotics |
| **A small ground-truth calibration set repairs overconfidence** | Simulation-based inference under a wrong simulator is overconfident; a small calibration set with known truth restored calibration, and was best even beyond 1,000 examples on a real task (Wehenkel 2024) | FULL_TEXT / ABSTRACT | tens to hundreds of truth-known cases | not diagnosis |
| **Fix the confident-wrong rate with a distribution-free bound** | Selective classification with a binomial upper bound sets the abstain threshold from labelled cases (Angelopoulos and Bates); per-class conformal coverage needs about 100 confirmed cases in the rarest class, 10 per class was off by 7.5 to 8.7 points (Ding et al.) | FULL_TEXT | a few hundred cases for marginal coverage; group rare causes | exchangeability assumption |
| **Label hygiene and a vocabulary built with practitioners** | Parts-replaced and closed-ticket labels are wrong a large share of the time (avionics no-fault-found 35 to 60%, 2005 interviews); 733 UPS log rows left 429 usable; synonyms moved one share 4/62 to 40/62 (Ademujimi and Prabhu) | mixed | interviews with plumbers before counting anything | different domains |
| **Low-burden confirmation capture, with field proxies that need no confirmed cause** | Designs converge on a delayed one-tap prompt with context prefilled (Bosch, Cummins patents); proxies: rank of the true cause, solution-found rate, repair actions and duration per failure (STMicroelectronics) | FULL_TEXT of patents | tool design; no response rates published | designs, not measured results |
| **Governed model updates** | Treat frequencies and reliabilities as Dirichlet/Beta priors with an explicit sample-size strength (Microsoft); expert accept or reject of each proposed change with its supporting cases (BAE); replay a fixed set of past visits against every revision (HP-family patent) | FULL_TEXT | review process | no published effect size |
| **Seeded-fault experiments for what passive logs cannot teach** | After 15 months and 304 incident reports only 1 of 23 elicited rates could be tested against records (Kornek 2024); the LBNL fault dataset's ground truth took functional tests plus expert inspection | FULL_TEXT | access to a cooperating building; safe, reversible faults | labour-intensive; signature fidelity rarely checked |
| **Structured expert elicitation done cheaply** | Pool several practitioners plus one discussion round beats individuals, but groups stay overconfident (90% intervals held the truth 36% of the time in one defence study; 80% intervals about 68% with the best format); seniority and peer reputation did not predict accuracy; clinicians overestimated base rates by roughly 2x to over 10x yet their sense of familiar tests' strength was often right | FULL_TEXT | 3 to 6 practitioners, a facilitator | not diagnostic networks specifically |
| **Value-of-information, not just sensitivity, to choose what to measure first** | In one worked portfolio, ranking by EVPI alone reversed the ranking by net research benefit; VOI-optimal trials were smaller than conventional ones (Tuffaha 2016) | FULL_TEXT | cost and uncertainty estimates per unknown | health economics |

## What the research says not to do

- **Do not train a classifier from field visits** at the volumes we can expect (more than 100 labelled faults per class are needed; with 63 real sequences accuracy was 14 to 18%).
- **Do not trust self-consistent validation.** Guideline 14-style fit metrics, simulated validation accuracy (96% then 69% real, secondhand) and simulation success (42.5 then 2.7 on hardware for hand-set randomization) all failed to predict real performance. Test on truth-known surrogate cases and score fit, parameter recovery and the answer separately.
- **Do not take vendor outcome claims at face value.** Dezide's "up to 70% faster" is a testimonial; Honeywell's airline figures were forecasts; no baseline anywhere.
- **Do not pick experts by seniority**, and do not ask them how often each cause occurs; ask about checks they do weekly.
- **Do not mine raw work orders for frequencies** without cleaning and a plumber-built vocabulary.

## Sizing: what to expect from a pilot

No literature gives a learning curve for our case. Two partial answers: one reader's own simulation (not a literature result: it assumes a skewed set of 17 true frequencies and a flattened expert prior) found about **100 confirmed visits identify the leading cause (99%) but the correct top three only 63% of the time**, and about **20 cases of a given cause reveal that an assumed check reliability is badly wrong**; rare causes' check reliabilities **will not be learned from passive visit logs** and need seeded-fault experiments. Per-class conformal coverage and classifier training both need around 100 per class. In our own simulation, knowing the cause frequencies exactly was worth about +0.05 top-1 (an earlier figure of +0.095 was corrected on 2026-10-10: it gave zero prior to causes absent from the test visits), a prior of unknown quality is expected to hurt (Addendum 11), and treating tenant answers as soft evidence mattered most when they were unreliable.

## Proposed order of work

**A. Before any pilot (in this repository and with a few interviews).**
1. Build the vocabulary of causes and symptoms with plumbers and building engineers (and use the manufacturer-document study's gaps: finer mixing-valve failure modes, hot-supply margin, indirect-tank primary side, over-temperature).
2. Specify and build the capture: one-tap confirmed cause after the expected job time, the readings, the arrival facts (especially cold-side static pressure), skipped steps flagged, and the tenant's answers next to what was found.
3. Build and test the learning loop on a **truth-known surrogate pilot** in simulation: a world that differs from the model in frequencies, check noise and tenant reliability; update base rates (Dirichlet with an explicit pseudo-count), one temperature, tenant and check reliabilities (Beta), and the selective-risk threshold; plot top-1, confident-wrong rate and calibration against 0, 20, 50, 100, 200 visits. This both sizes the pilot and is software we need anyway. *This is the next thing I would build.*
4. Write the shadow-mode protocol: log the confirmed cause for all visits, record case mix, replay a fixed visit set against every model revision.

**B. A cooperating building or two (days, no faults needed).**
5. Normal-operation survey: the checks that matter on healthy plants (heater outlet, principal and riser returns, loop dT, time to hot, static pressure, pump amps) to calibrate what "normal" looks like and replace the simulator's absolute values.
6. Pooled elicitation of check reliabilities and times from 3 to 6 technicians with a discussion round; widen the intervals they give.

**C. A service-company partner (first 50 to 100 labelled visits).**
7. Recalibrate base rates and temperature; measure tenant reliability directly (tenant answer against what was found); fix the confident-wrong rate with a distribution-free bound; replay and review each update; keep "other" alive.

**D. Later.**
8. Safe, reversible seeded faults (a throttled balancing valve, a shifted aquastat) for rare causes and for the check reliabilities passive logs cannot reach; use value of information to decide which unknown to measure next.

## What this research cannot tell us

- How much real accuracy any of these steps adds in hot-water plants: no source measures it, and the closest comparable gaps in the literature are 20 to 45 points (96.8% simulated to 70% real for a robot digital-twin diagnostic; near 100% to 54 to 63% AUROC when whole units are held out), which our own 0.76 versus 0.51 gap sits inside.
- How many field measurements Bayesian calibration needs: the building papers use anywhere from 12 monthly values to thousands of minute-level points; the answer depends on identifiability, not count.
- Whether expert-elicited probabilities or field-updated ones do better in troubleshooting networks: no published comparison.
